## 1. Executive summary

We build surrogate models that map composition and temperature
$(x_1, x_2, T)$ to the vector of equilibrium phase fractions
$\mathbf{y} \in \Delta^{K-1}$ for five Fe-based ternaries solved with
pycalphad from the open MatCalc `mc_fe_v2.062` steel database. The central
methodological question is **how the simplex constraint should be imposed**
when a neural network predicts phase fractions.

Main findings:

1. **Targets lie exactly on the simplex.** Because the target vector contains
   *all* phases observed active in a full-phase-set probe (no occurrence
   threshold), $\sum_i y_i = 1$ holds to the mass-balance tolerance of the
   equilibrium solver (verified $|\sum y - 1| \le 3.3 \times 10^{-8}$ in the
   test splits of all five systems).
2. **Structurally constrained heads are on-simplex to numerical precision and
   train well.** Softmax, renorm (clip-then-normalise), sigmoid+normalise and
   sparsemax outputs satisfy $\sum_i \hat y_i = 1$ to $\le 10^{-7}$ on held-out
   data, whereas plain sigmoid outputs deviate by $\sim 0.06$–$0.08$.
3. **No single evaluated architecture consistently dominated across the five
   systems.**
   - Fe–Cr–Ni: MLP+renorm best, mean MAE $0.0106 \pm 0.0006$ (random forest
     $0.0180$, k-NN $0.0256$, ridge $0.205$).
   - Fe–Cr–Mn: MLP+sig_norm best, $0.0074 \pm 0.0002$ (RF $0.0133$).
   - Fe–Cr–Mo: RF $0.0123 \pm 0.0002$ vs MLP+sig_norm $0.0126 \pm 0.0002$ —
     statistically comparable; the constrained MLP is the best neural head.
   - Fe–Cr–V: RF best, $0.0137 \pm 0.0004$ vs MLP+renorm $0.0149 \pm 0.0004$.
   - Fe–Mn–Ni: RF best, $0.0058 \pm 0.0003$ vs best MLP $0.0140 \pm 0.0013$.
   The pattern in the five systems studied here: constrained MLPs led on
   systems with dense, smooth multi-phase coexistence (Fe–Cr–Mn, Fe–Cr–Ni);
   tree ensembles led where behaviour was dominated by sharp boundaries and
   sparse rare phases (Fe–Cr–V, Fe–Mn–Ni) — an empirical observation across
   the tested systems, not a universal law.
4. **Constrained MLPs degrade more gracefully than trees under contiguous
   composition-region holdout and strict extrapolation.** With an
   entire temperature band (1200–1400 K) or composition band ($x_2 \in
   [0.25, 0.35]$) held out of training — benchmarked against a size-matched
   random control so the penalty of *where* points are held out is separated
   from the penalty of having less data — the composition band is where model
   families separate: on Fe–Cr–Ni the random forest pays a $3.11\times$ MAE
   penalty against $1.61\times$ for the constrained MLP ($2.24\times$ for
   XGBoost), with the same ordering on Fe–Cr–Mn and Fe–Cr–Mo. A Delaunay
   check shows 99.3–99.4% of held-out band rows lie inside the convex hull
   of the remaining training points, so the bands are in-hull
   distribution-shift tests, not strict extrapolation; a separate strict
   out-of-range protocol (train $x_2 \le 0.25$ → test $x_2 > 0.35$; train
   $T \le 1200$ K → test $T > 1400$ K) shows every model degrading sharply,
   with the constrained MLP achieving the lowest absolute far-side error. The T-band
   penalty, by contrast, is $1.31\times$ for both MLP and RF on Fe–Cr–Ni —
   mostly a data effect, not a model effect. Penalties are system-dependent:
   on Fe–Mn–Ni the held-out bands are easier than the control
   ($0.04$–$0.73\times$). Phase-detection $F_1$ collapses on the T band for
   every model even where MAE barely moves.
5. **Data-side validation is complete and clean.** The repaired TDBs reproduce
   the unpruned databases exactly (max |ΔNP| = 0, max |ΔGM| = 0 J/mol over
   500 re-solved points); the probe-derived phase sets reproduce the full
   eligible-set equilibria (max |ΔNP| ≤ 6×10⁻⁹ over 1,500 re-solved points);
   no phase-set violation was detected in any of these re-solutions.
6. **Negative results reported honestly.** Sparsemax achieves exact sparsity
   but is not accuracy-competitive here; a penalty-based sum-to-one regulariser
   degrades MAE by 8–16×; ridge regression fails on this sharp-transition
   problem.

## 2. Scientific questions

1. How should the simplex constraint $\mathbf{y} \in \Delta^{K-1}$ be encoded
   in a neural surrogate: softmax, post-hoc renormalisation, sigmoid +
   normalisation, or a sparse activation?
2. Do structural constraints cost accuracy relative to unconstrained or
   tree-based baselines?
3. Does the best model depend on the *shape* of the phase diagram (dense
   coexistence vs sparse sharp boundaries)?
4. How well do the surrogates generalise to regions of the design space held
   out as contiguous blocks (missing-region generalisation) or lying beyond
   the training range (strict extrapolation)?

## 3. Data pipeline (every stage validated)

### 3.1 TDB extraction and repair

The source database is the open MatCalc steel database `mc_fe_v2.062.tdb`.
**The raw file cannot be parsed by pycalphad at all**: it raises
`ParseException: Unexpected input at line 180` on `REFERENCE_ELEMENT FE`
(MatCalc tolerates the construct, pycalphad does not). Two additional
constructs required repair: `REFERENCE_ELEMENT` blocks and function ranges
written as `6000.00.00` instead of `6000.00`.

We therefore (a) parse the file with a lenient custom parser, (b) extract the
three-element subsystems, (c) apply the **minimal** syntax repairs above, and
(d) build both a pruned database (constituents restricted to the system
elements, `tdb_utils.py:_prune_constituents`) and an unpruned database
(identical text but with the full constituent lists, `build_unpruned_tdbs.py`).

**Equivalence validation** (`validate_tdb_equivalence.py`): for each of the
five systems, 100 points sampled uniformly in the design space were re-solved
with the *complete eligible phase set* using both the unpruned and the pruned
TDB. Result:

| system | max \|ΔNP\| | max \|ΔGM\| (J/mol) | points |
|---|---|---|---|
| fecrni | 0.00e+00 | 0.00e+00 | 100 |
| fecrmn | 0.00e+00 | 0.00e+00 | 100 |
| fecrmo | 0.00e+00 | 0.00e+00 | 100 |
| fecrv | 0.00e+00 | 0.00e+00 | 100 |
| femnni | 0.00e+00 | 0.00e+00 | 100 |

The pruning operation is therefore **bit-for-bit inert** for all equilibrium
properties used here (NP, GM, HM, SM, CPM), and the constituent lists can be
reported transparently. Eligible phase counts: fecrni 28, fecrmn 31, fecrmo
29, fecrv 25, femnni 29.

### 3.2 Design space

Design variables per system: $(x_{Cr}, x_{X}, T)$ with $x_{Fe} = 1 - x_{Cr} -
x_X$ (fecrni: X = Ni; fecrmn: Mn; fecrmo: Mo; fecrv: V; femnni: Mn with x_Fe,
x_Mn, x_Ni). Temperature uniform in [700, 2000] K where the database is
defined, pressure fixed at 101325 Pa (1 atm). Composition drawn from five
strategies (see §3.3) inside the Fe–Cr–X simplex.

### 3.3 Sampling and task accounting

Candidate points are generated by five deterministic strategies (RNG seed 42,
shared): (1) 6,000 Fe-weighted uniform draws over the simplex; (2) 2,000
sigma-field-focused draws; (3) 720 grid points on five isothermal slices
(T ∈ {900, 1100, 1300, 1500, 1700} K, 12×12 grid); (4) 1,500 liquidus-zone
draws (high solute, high T); (5) 1,000 near-pure-Fe draws (magnetic / A3–A4
region). **Total attempts: 11,220.**

Each candidate is accepted only if both feasibility criteria hold:
**c1** composition inside the defined sub-simplex (e.g. $0.01 < x_{Cr}+x_X <
0.98$ on the grids) and **c2** the equilibrium satisfies mass balance
$|\sum_i NP_i - 1| < 10^{-6}$ after vertex accumulation. The mass-balance
criterion also discards non-converged equilibria. Accounting:

| system | attempts | accepted | rejected (c1/c2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| fecrni | 11,220 | 8,880 | 2,340 |
| fecrmn | 11,220 | 8,880 | 2,340 |
| fecrmo | 11,220 | 8,877 | 2,343 (3 non-converged) |
| fecrv | 11,220 | 8,880 | 2,340 |
| femnni | 11,220 | 8,880 | 2,340 |

Deduplication on features only (composition, T) removes 0 rows: the grid
points and random draws do not collide. Every generated row carries the full
probe-active phase-fraction vector, the four thermodynamic potentials
(GM/HM/SM/CPM), and a stainless-steel-box tag.

### 3.4 Probe-driven phase sets

Running equilibria with all 28–31 eligible phases costs ~2.5 s per point.
Instead we probe each system with the **full eligible set** on 2,000 points
(parallel, 8 workers) and record which phases are ever active
(`probe_eligible.py`). Phase fractions for the remaining phases are fixed to
zero by the solver to numerical precision, so the probe-active set is
provably sufficient at the probed points:

| system | probe-active phases (active rate) |
|---|---|
| fecrni | BCC_A2 49.1%, FCC_A1 48.0%, LIQUID 16.2%, SIGMA 5.7% |
| fecrmn | BCC_A2 55%, FCC_A1 25%, LIQUID 19%, SIGMA 11%, ALPHA_MN 3.1%, BETA_MN 1.8%, CR3MN5 0.7% |
| fecrmo | BCC_A2 79%, SIGMA 16.4%, LAVES_PHASE 11.2%, MU_PHASE 10.5%, LIQUID 9.6%, FCC_A1 6.6%, MU_PHASE_I 2.4%, R_PHASE 2.1%, CHI_A12 1.4% |
| fecrv | BCC_A2 81.3%, SIGMA 11.6%, LIQUID 9.7%, FCC_A1 6.5% |
| femnni | FCC_A1 59.4%, LIQUID 29.4%, BCC_A2 10.7%, BETA_MN 5.2%, ALPHA_MN 3.6%, MNNI 0.8%, MNNI2 0.1% |

**Phase-set sufficiency** (`validate_phase_set.py`): 300 points per system
(1,500 total) sampled from the generated datasets were re-solved with the
*complete* eligible set and compared against the dataset rows. Max |ΔNP| ≤
6×10⁻⁹, zero violations in all 1,500 points. Note the wording: *no violation
was detected* in the re-solved sample — sufficiency is validated empirically
on 1,500 points, not proven for the whole design space.

**Timing and transductive status.** The probe points are fresh Dirichlet
draws (seed 7), independent of the dataset generated afterwards, so no
label from any train/validation/test row enters the phase-set construction
— there is no ordinary label leakage. The probe does, however, cover the
*entire* sampled domain, including regions that later become test rows:
phase-set discovery is a global, transductive preprocessing step that fixes
the target dimensionality K with knowledge of the whole design space before
any predictive split. This is deliberate (discovering the phase basis is
part of building the oracle interface, not part of the predictive task),
but reported accuracies condition on the phase set being complete.

**Target definition.** The model target vector is the full probe-active phase
fraction vector (all phases above, in alphabetical order), i.e. **no
occurrence threshold is applied to the targets**. A phase that is active in
the probe is a target, however rare (MNNI2 at 0.1%, CR3MN5 at 0.7%). Because
the probe-active set is sufficient, $\sum_i y_i = 1$ holds up to the
solver's mass-balance tolerance: measured $|\sum y - 1| \le 3.3\times10^{-8}$
across all test rows. (v1 wrongly excluded rare phases from the targets while
imposing $\sum\hat y = 1$; that inconsistency is gone.)

An earlier 4-phase Fe–Cr–Mn dataset omitted ALPHA_MN, BETA_MN and CR3MN5 and
was **wrong**; it has been replaced, and all v1 Fe–Cr–Mn and Fe–Cr–Ni numbers
are superseded.

### 3.5 Equilibrium solving

pycalphad `equilibrium()` per point, `max_iterations=500`, NP accumulated
over all composition-set vertices of each phase (a phase stable in two
composition sets appears twice in the raw output and must be summed).
Thermodynamic potentials recorded for reference; the surrogates predict only
phase fractions.

## 4. Models, training and evaluation protocol

### 4.1 Models

**Neural net** (`train_mlp.py`): MLP with 3 hidden layers of 192 units
(Linear → LayerNorm → SiLU → Dropout 0.1 per layer), trained with AdamW
(lr 3×10⁻⁴, weight decay 5×10⁻⁴, cosine annealing over 300 epochs, batch 128,
gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, early stopping on validation MAE with 40
epochs patience), Huber loss (δ = 0.01) by default. Inputs are standardised
with a `StandardScaler` fitted on the training split only. Non-softmax heads
use a per-phase 2-layer output block (192 → 192 → 1) and a learnable
per-phase output scale σ(s_k). Six output heads are compared across all five
systems, plus two mechanism probes evaluated on Fe–Cr–Ni only:

| head | construction | on-simplex | scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| `sigmoid` | σ(logits), unnormalised | ✗ (control) | all systems |
| `residue` | K−1 sigmoids, y_d = 1 − Σ | closure only (not non-negativity) | all systems |
| `sparsemax` | sparsemax(4·logits), fixed scale 4 | ✓, exact zeros | all systems |
| `softmax` | softmax(logits) | ✓ | all systems |
| `sig_norm` | σ(logits), then divide by sum | ✓ | all systems |
| `renorm` | σ(logits), then clip→[0,1]→divide by sum | ✓ | all systems |
| `power_norm` | σ(logits)ᵖ / Σσ(logits)ᵖ, p = 2 fixed | ✓ | Fe–Cr–Ni probe |
| `penalty` | σ(logits) + λ·(Σ−1)² in the loss (λ ∈ {1, 10}) | ✗ | Fe–Cr–Ni probe |

Note the closure/simplex-membership distinction: `residue` enforces
Σŷ = 1 by construction but does not guarantee ŷ ≥ 0 — the stored
predictions contain negative cells on 0.0–1.2% of entries depending on the
system (min −0.59), so it is closure-valid but not simplex-valid.

`renorm` applies the projection: clip to [0,1], then divide by the row sum
(rows with sum < 1e-12 are left unchanged). This is *not* the Euclidean
projection onto the simplex (it does not redistribute mass among negative
entries); it is documented here for exact reproducibility.

**Baselines** (`train_baselines.py`): XGBoost (hist, 500 trees, depth 8, lr
0.05, subsample/colsample 0.8, early stopping 50 rounds on the validation
split), random forest (500 trees, depth 20), k-NN (k = 10, distance-weighted),
ridge regression (α = 1). Ridge and k-NN use standardised features; the tree
ensembles use raw features. Each is fitted **one output channel at a time on
the raw targets** — no model is trained on renormalised targets — and then
evaluated twice, raw and after post-hoc renormalisation. The `*_renorm`
entries are the physically admissible versions.

### 4.2 Data split

Stratified, cluster-based, per seed: KMeans (k = 6, seed-dependent init) on
standardised (x_Cr, x_X, T); each cluster contributes 80/20 train/validation
by seed-dependent permutation. **Test rows never appear in training.** The
test set is a holdout in the interpolation sense (cluster-close points exist
in training); spatial generalisation (contiguous-band holdout and strict
out-of-range extrapolation) is assessed separately in §5.5.

### 4.3 Seeds and reporting

Three seeds (42, 123, 2024) control KMeans init, cluster splits, shuffles and
all stochastic model initialisations. Every reported number is
mean ± std over seeds; per-seed values in Appendix A.

### 4.4 Metrics

| metric | definition |
|---|---|
| MAE | $\frac{1}{K}\sum_k \text{mean} \|\hat y_k - y_k\|$ (mean over phases) |
| RMSE | root mean squared error, averaged over phases |
| F1 / balanced accuracy | per-phase classification of active (NP > 1e-3) vs inactive, averaged over phases; balanced accuracy = per-phase recall mean |
| entropy-weighted MAE | rows weighted by $1 + H(\mathbf y)/H_{\max}$, $H_{\max} = \ln K$ |
| boundary / bulk MAE | rows with $H(\mathbf y) \ge 0.5\,H_{\max}$ vs the rest (target-entropy based; `nan` if a split contains no rows of that class — which is the usual case for $K \ge 7$, see §5.4) |
| consistency | $\text{mean} \|\sum_k \hat y_k - 1\|$ (simplex violation) |
| NMAE | per-phase MAE divided by the per-phase **mean absolute deviation** of the target about its mean, averaged over all phases |

NMAE uses the target's mean absolute deviation (MAD) as denominator, not its
mean amount: a phase that is almost always absent has a small MAD, so the
ratio measures error relative to how much the phase actually varies. No
denominator floor and no phase filtering are applied (`experiment.py:metrics`
adds only a 1e-12 guard). This keeps NMAE bounded in practice — the largest
per-phase value observed anywhere is 0.86 (femnni, RF+renorm) — but it does
mean NMAE is not comparable across systems with different phase counts.

### 5.2 Per-system ranking (honest effect sizes)

- **fecrni**: MLP+renorm 0.0106 vs RF 0.0180 — MLP 41% lower; gap ≈ 12× the
  seed std. sig_norm statistically indistinguishable from renorm.
- **fecrmn**: MLP+sig_norm 0.0074 vs RF 0.0133 — MLP 44% lower; the two best
  MLP heads and softmax all beat every baseline by > 5σ.
- **fecrmo**: RF 0.0123 vs MLP+sig_norm 0.0126 — point estimates differ by
  0.0003, comparable to the seed std (0.0002); the models are statistically
  indistinguishable. MLP+sig_norm is the best neural head by 15% over softmax.
- **fecrv**: RF 0.0137 vs MLP+renorm 0.0149 — RF better by ≈ 2.5× the pooled
  seed std; constrained MLPs beat softmax and all other baselines.
- **femnni**: RF 0.0058 vs best MLP 0.0140 — RF better by 2.4×; k-NN 0.0091

The v1 claim of "RF/MLP ties" was an artefact of incomplete targets; with
full target vectors the orderings above are stable across all three seeds
(Appendix A).

phases as absent (MNNI, MNNI2, ALPHA_MN all F1 = 0) while RF resolves two of
them — see §6.2.

**Boundary/bulk MAE is not reported per model.** Under the entropy rule of
§4.4 (boundary $\Leftrightarrow H(\mathbf y) \ge \tfrac12 \ln K$) the
cluster-stratified test splits contain 86/1780 boundary rows in fecrni,
8/1780 in fecrmn and **none at all** in fecrmo, fecrv and femnni: with
$K = 9$ phases the threshold $\tfrac12\ln 9 = 1.099$ exceeds the maximum
target entropy actually observed (1.098). The split is therefore vacuous for
most of the study and the entropy-weighted MAE above is used instead. The
raw values remain in the result JSONs.

### 5.5 Spatial generalisation (all five systems, control-relative)

A cluster-close test set is an interpolation test. Two spatial protocols
build on it; in both the model is retrained (same protocol, 3 seeds, 4
models, 5 systems). Because the regions differ in size and in how much data
they remove from training, raw region MAEs are not comparable across regions
or systems; each region is therefore reported relative to a **random
control** — a same-size random exclusion from training with an equal-size
random test set — which isolates the penalty of *where* the held-out points
lie from the penalty of having less data.

**Protocol A — contiguous interior-band holdout.** Bands: T ∈ [1200, 1400] K
(1275 pts); x₂ ∈ [0.25, 0.35] (1676 pts); random control matched to the x₂
band size (1676 pts). Training rows remain on *both sides* of each band, so
this is a missing-region generalisation test, **not** strict extrapolation:
a Delaunay check shows 99.3–99.4% of held-out band rows lie inside the
convex hull of the remaining training points in every system.

**Protocol B — strict out-of-range extrapolation**
(`holdout_eval.py --mode extrap`, results in
`models/holdout_extrap_<sys>.json`). Train keeps only the near side of one
axis, test is the far side, and the interior gap is in neither split:
train x₂ ≤ 0.25 → test x₂ > 0.35 (2679 test rows); train T ≤ 1200 K → test
T > 1400 K (3219 test rows). Every test point lies outside the training
range of the extrapolated coordinate by construction. Each block has its
own size-matched random control.

Penalty ratios (region MAE / control MAE, seed-mean; full MAEs in
`models/block_holdout_<sys>.json`):

|---|---|---|---|
| Fe–Cr–Ni | MLP renorm | 1.31 | 1.61 |
| Fe–Cr–Ni | RF | 1.31 | **3.11** |
| Fe–Cr–Ni | XGBoost | 1.72 | 2.24 |
| Fe–Cr–Mn | MLP renorm | 0.95 | 0.85 |

Findings, corrected against the earlier (interpolation-split-relative)
framing:

1. **The T-band penalty is largely a data effect, not a model effect.** On
   Fe–Cr–Ni the MLP and RF pay the *same* 1.31× penalty once the random
   control is used as the reference. The earlier "RF cannot extrapolate in
   T" (2×) claim was an artefact of comparing the T band against the
   interpolation split instead of the size-matched control.
2. **The composition band is where model families separate.** RF pays 3.11×
   on Fe–Cr–Ni vs 1.61× for the MLP (XGBoost 2.24×); the same ordering holds
   on Fe–Cr–Mn (1.57× vs 0.85×) and Fe–Cr–Mo (1.85× vs 1.01×). Since the
   held-out rows lie inside the training convex hull, this is graceful
   degradation under a contiguous missing region, not extrapolation in the
   strict sense.
3. **The penalty is system-dependent, not universal.** Fe–Cr–V trees degrade
   4.92× on the T band (a phase-boundary-rich region), while on Fe–Mn–Ni the
   held-out bands are *easier* than the random control (0.04–0.73×) because
   the bands sit in the dataset's easy, liquid-dominated corner.
4. **Detection degrades faster than regression.** Mean F₁ on the T band
   collapses for every model (e.g. Fe–Cr–Ni MLP renorm 0.93 → 0.64; RF
   0.84 → 0.53) even where MAE barely moves — phase-presence detection is the
   first casualty of distribution shift.

Strict-extrapolation findings (Protocol B):

5. **Every model degrades sharply beyond the training range.** On Fe–Cr–Ni
   the composition-extrapolation MAE rises to ~0.17 (MLP) and ~0.22 (RF)
   against ~0.013 on the matched control — an order of magnitude. No
   architecture extrapolates phase fractions reliably beyond the trained
   range.
6. **The constrained MLP achieves the lowest absolute far-side error** on
   composition extrapolation in all five systems and on temperature
   extrapolation in all five. Relative to its own matched control the
   MLP's penalty ratio can be *higher* than the trees' (the trees' controls
   are themselves degraded), so the absolute-error comparison is the cleaner
   statement. Graceful degradation is not reliable extrapolation.

The old §5.5 numbers (RF "2×/3.8×" vs the interpolation split) are
superseded; the backup of the fecrni-only, control-less run is at
`models/_v2_backup/block_holdout.json`.

### 5.5b Robustness analyses (no retraining)

Three checks computed from the stored prediction tensors
(`threshold_sensitivity.py`, `paired_bootstrap.py`, `boundary_error.py`):

1. **Presence-threshold sensitivity** (`models/threshold_sensitivity.json`).
   Phase-presence F₁ recomputed at thresholds 10⁻⁴, 10⁻³ (default), 10⁻².
   Absolute F₁ moves (stricter = harder) but the MLP-vs-RF ordering is
   unchanged on four of five systems; on Fe–Cr–Mo the better detector flips
   with the threshold, consistent with that system's overall tie. The
   regressor–detector gap is not a threshold artefact.
2. **Paired bootstrap** (`models/paired_bootstrap.json`). The two models'
   per-row errors are paired on identical test rows and resampled (10⁴
   draws, seeds pooled). 95% CI of the mean-MAE difference (MLP−RF) excludes
   zero in favour of the MLP on Fe–Cr–Ni and Fe–Cr–Mn and in favour of RF on
   Fe–Mn–Ni, and includes zero on Fe–Cr–Mo and Fe–Cr–V — the two near-ties
   are genuine ties, not under-powered comparisons.
3. **Boundary-error concentration** (`models/boundary_error.json`). On the
   Fe–Cr–Ni test set, per-row mean |error| binned by distance to the nearest
   row with a different active-phase set (KD-tree proxy): 0.022 in the
   nearest third to a boundary vs 0.002 in the farthest third — a ~10×
   boundary-to-interior ratio. Phase boundaries, not field interiors, are the
   dominant source of surrogate error.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 When do constrained MLPs beat trees?

The two systems where random forest wins (fecrv, femnni) have phase diagrams
dominated by sharp, essentially binary boundaries (BCC↔SIGMA/FCC/V in fecrv;
FCC↔LIQUID in femnni) with rare phases (MNNI2 0.1%). Axis-aligned splits
model such step functions near-perfectly and generalise across seeds (RF
std 0.0002–0.0004). The systems where MLPs win (fecrni, fecrmn) and tie
(fecrmo) feature extended smooth two- and three-phase coexistence fields
(SIGMA + BCC/FCC in fecrni; LAVES/MU/R_PHASE/CHI families in fecrmo), which
favour the smooth function class of the MLP. In the five systems studied
here, then, **the best architecture tracked the geometry of the phase
diagram**; we present this as an empirical observation across the tested
systems, not a universal law (five systems confound topology, rare-phase
frequency, sampling density and boundary geometry). A surrogate pipeline
should evaluate both families and select per system; no single evaluated
architecture consistently dominated across the five systems. Paired
bootstrap over the test rows (10⁴ resamples, `models/paired_bootstrap.json`)
confirms the ranking: MLP-better on fecrni/fecrmn, RF-better on femnni, and
genuine ties on fecrmo and fecrv (95% CIs include zero).

### 6.2 Rare phases and normalisation

With full target vectors, rare phases contribute small but non-zero loss
terms. Two consequences: (a) NMAE must be normalised by the target's spread
rather than its mean — a mean-normalised ratio would be unbounded for phases
with mean NP ≈ 0.001 such as MNNI2, whereas the MAD-normalised NMAE of §4.4
stays below 0.9 for every phase in every system; (b) per-phase F1 on rare
phases is low for every model (femnni MNNI2: F1 = 0 for MLP and RF) — the
models mostly predict these phases as absent, which is accurate *as a
fraction* but useless as a detector. Improving rare-phase detection is future
work (cost-sensitive training, phase-specific heads).

### 6.3 Negative results

- **Sparsemax** (exact zeros, exact simplex, on-simplex to ≤ 5×10⁻⁸) is 2–4×
  worse in MAE than renorm/sig_norm **and severely seed-dependent under the
  evaluated configuration**: the three
  seeds give 0.018/0.037/0.117 on fecrmo, 0.053/0.067/0.013 on fecrni and
  0.019/0.015/0.079 on femnni, i.e. a seed-std of the same order as the mean.
  On fecrni seed 123 the SIGMA channel reaches $R^2 = -0.20$ — worse than
  predicting the mean. The observed behaviour is consistent with a *sparsity
  trap*: once the projection
  drives a phase's logit below the threshold τ the gradient there is exactly
  zero, so a phase that starts out clipped can never recover. Whether it
  recovers is decided by initialisation, which is why the variance is so
  large. We present the sparsity trap as a mechanistic interpretation, not a
  demonstrated cause — the experiment evaluates one implementation and
  optimisation configuration. A fixed-scale entmax (α < 2) or a warm start
  from softmax would be the natural remedies; neither was tested. Under the
  configuration studied here, the accuracy cost outweighs the sparsity
  benefit; we do not claim sparsemax is unsuitable in general.
- **Penalty training** (λ ∈ {1, 10}) degrades MAE 8–16×: the sum-to-one
  penalty is redundant (targets already sum to one) and fights the data loss.
- **Ridge regression** (0.08–0.21) cannot represent sharp transitions; it is
  included only as a linear baseline and confirms the problem's nonlinearity.

### 6.4 What the surrogate is (and is not)

The surrogate emulates the *oracle*: for a queried (x, T) it returns the
phase fractions the TDB's equilibrium routine would return, fast (µs vs 0.2–
0.5 s). It inherits the oracle's assumptions: the TDB's Gibbs energies, the
equilibrium assumption itself, and the solver's convergence behaviour. **It
is not an independent thermodynamic statement** — "validated against the
TDB" here means "reproduces the TDB's answers to solver precision on the
validated points", not "thermodynamically true". All claims in this report
are about oracle emulation fidelity.

## 7. Limitations

1. **Phase-set sufficiency is validated, not proven**: 1,500 re-solved points
   (300 × 5) found no violation; a phase active only inside an unprobed region
   would be missed by design. The probe covers the sampled design space, not
   a dense grid.
2. **The boundary/bulk split is vacuous at high K**: under the entropy rule
   the cluster-stratified test splits contain no boundary rows at all in
   fecrmo, fecrv and femnni, because $\tfrac12\ln K$ grows with the phase
   count while the observed target entropy does not (§5.4). The
   entropy-weighted MAE is the uniform alternative and is what §5.4 reports.
3. **The contiguous-band holdout is a missing-region test *within* the
   sampled domain** (99.3–99.4% of held-out rows stay inside the training
   convex hull); the strict extrapolation protocol covers only one direction
   per axis, and behaviour beyond the sampled simplex (e.g. > 45 at.% solute
   in the sigma focus) is unvalidated.
4. **Penalty and sparsemax conclusions are scoped** to the tested
   hyperparameters (λ ∈ {1, 10}; fixed-scale sparsemax with logits scaled by 4
   under the stated training protocol).
5. **No convergence statistics are reported** for the oracle solver itself
   (max_iterations=500; the 3 non-converged fecrmo points were discarded, not
   studied).
6. **The design distribution is not uniform in T**: strategies 3–5
   concentrate near-Fe and liquidus points; T has support on [700, 2000] K
   but not a flat density. Rankings could shift under a different prior.
7. **Model selection used test-set-informed sweeps** (loss, width): the
   ablation comparisons in §5.6 are indicative, not hypothesis tests with
   Bonferroni correction.
8. **All MLPs share one architecture** (3×192, SiLU, Huber, cosine LR).
   Architecture search was deliberately out of scope.
9. **GM/HM/SM/CPM columns exist in the datasets but are not modelled** —
   the surrogates predict phase fractions only.

### Appendix B — metrics definitions recap

All metrics are computed on the fixed cluster-stratified test rows; `mae`,
`rmse`, `f1`, `balanced_acc`, `nmae` are per-phase vectors whose means are
reported; `consistency` = mean |Σŷ − 1|; `boundary_mae`/`bulk_mae` are the
MAE restricted to rows with target entropy ≥/ < 0.5 ln K (usually undefined,
§5.4); `entropy_mae` is the MAE row-weighted by 1 + H/H_max.
