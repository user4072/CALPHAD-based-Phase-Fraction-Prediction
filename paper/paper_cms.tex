\title{Simplex Constraints, Spatial Generalization, and the
Regressor--Detector Gap in Machine-Learning Surrogates for CALPHAD
Phase-Fraction Prediction}

\author[a]{Anonymous}
\address[a]{Anonymous}

\begin{abstract}
We benchmark simplex-constraint mechanisms for machine-learning surrogates
mapping composition and temperature to CALPHAD phase fractions: six neural
heads and four baselines on five Fe-based ternaries (44{,}397 equilibria)
plus quaternary and carbide extensions. Softmax, sigmoid/$\Sigma$ and
clip-renormalise close to $\le 5\times10^{-8}$ at no accuracy cost;
projection alone recovers renorm accuracy to $\le 1.3\times10^{-4}$.
Under fraction-only supervision the ranking tracks phase geometry --- but
a presence-gated head, which additionally sees presence labels, leads the
forest on all seven systems while lifting macro-AUPRC from 0.36--0.99 to
0.91--0.99. Spatial protocols with random and region-matched controls, and
three negative results (sparsemax exhibits severe seed sensitivity; a
sum-to-one penalty degrades MAE; the residue head is 1.4--2.5 times worse),
complete the benchmark. Against four DTA transitions the surrogate matches
CALPHAD within 21~K (mean 17.75~K), the same order as the database's 19~K
deviation from experiment. A 501{,}501-composition screen shortlists
10{,}936 candidates in 0.4~s; all 10{,}936 shortlist points confirm.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Phase fractions as a regression target}

The CALPHAD method predicts equilibrium phase assemblages from assessed
thermodynamic databases \citep{saunders1998calphad,lukas2007computational}.
Given a database, the equilibrium at fixed composition $\mathbf{x}$,
temperature $T$ and pressure $P$ follows from constrained Gibbs-energy
minimisation \citep{connolly2017primer}, and the output of interest in alloy design is frequently the
vector of phase fractions $\mathbf{y} = (\Np{1}, \ldots, \Np{K})$, where
$K$ is the number of phases that can appear. This vector lies on the
probability simplex,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{y} \in \Dk, \qquad
    \sum_{k=1}^{K} \Np{k} = 1, \qquad \Np{k} \ge 0,
    \label{eq:simplex}
\end{equation}
by the mass balance of the equilibrium solver. Any surrogate that predicts
$\mathbf{y}$ should respect Eq.~\eqref{eq:simplex}; a prediction that
violates it is not merely inaccurate but physically inadmissible.

Machine-learning surrogates replace the per-point minimisation with a forward
pass \citep{hart2021fast}, within the broader Integrated Computational
Materials Engineering and materials-informatics programmes
\citep{wang2019icme,panchal2013key,agrawal2016perspective,ward2017review}.
Surrogates of phase-equilibrium and
quasi-equilibrium calculations have been developed for process optimisation
and phase-field coupling \citep{nentwich2019surrogate,jiang2019fast,eiken2006multiphase},
deep-learning surrogates now reproduce CALPHAD phase fractions fast enough
to screen composition spaces spanning more than ten elements
\citep{tahkola2026accelerated}, and neighbouring efforts target
phase-diagram construction \citep{xi2025phasediagram}, high-entropy phase
fractions \citep{liu2024hea}, phase constitution in multicomponent alloys
\citep{vazquez2023dnn}, steel phase volume fractions by unsupervised
learning \citep{kim2021phasevol}, counterfactual steel design
\citep{xie2026knowledge}, Fe-based hardfacing alloys \citep{yu2026hardfacing},
unsupervised high-entropy alloy design \citep{lis2026unsupervised}, and
accelerated flash calculations \citep{zhang2020flash}.
Machine-learning models of phase formation
and transformation temperatures are widely used in alloy design
\citep{feng2021general,lu2024classified}, high-throughput microstructure
simulation in compositionally complex alloys builds on the same foundations
\citep{liy2021micro}, and uncertainty quantification for
CALPHAD-based workflows is an active companion topic
\citep{wang2020uncertainty}. For phase fractions the modelling question
decomposes into two: how to represent the output so that
Eq.~\eqref{eq:simplex} holds, and whether the representation costs accuracy.
Existing studies adopt a particular representation --- typically softmax or
post-hoc renormalisation --- but to our knowledge no study has systematically
compared simplex-constraint mechanisms across multiple alloy systems; the
present benchmark addresses that gap.

One distinction frames everything that follows. A surrogate learns the map
$(\mathbf{x}, T) \to \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{CALPHAD}}$: it reproduces what the
database's equilibrium routine returns, not what physical truth would
return. ``Validated against CALPHAD'' therefore means ``reproduces the
database/solver outputs to solver precision'', not ``independently
validates the thermodynamic database''. Every accuracy claim in this paper
is a statement about emulation fidelity, not thermodynamic truth.

\subsection{Generalisation beyond the training split, and the control
problem}

A second question concerns generalisation. The standard train/test split ---
random or cluster-stratified --- tests \emph{interpolation}: every test point
has training neighbours in $(\mathbf{x}, T)$. Alloy design, however, queries
points far from the computed ones, in two distinct senses. First, a
\emph{contiguous interior region} may be missing from the training data ---
a hole in the middle of the sampled domain, with training points on all
sides. Second, a query may lie \emph{outside the range} of one of the training
coordinates altogether --- strict, one-sided extrapolation along that
coordinate. The two are
different tests: in the first the test points typically remain inside the
convex hull of the training set, in the second they do not. Both protocols
share a confound --- removing the held-out region also removes training
data --- which Section~\ref{sec:protocols} resolves with size-matched
random controls and a region-matched reanalysis. Out-of-distribution
generalisation is an active concern across materials machine learning
\citep{li2025ood,segal2025ood}: tabular models degrade under geometric
shift \citep{lix2025transfer}, generative approaches are explored for
extrapolation \citep{hatakeyama2021generative}, and statistical
extrapolation carries risk even for physics-based models
\citep{speckhard2025extrapolation}.

\subsection{Contributions}

The study is deliberately scoped to small systems --- five ternaries plus
one quaternary and one carbide-rich ternary as scope extensions --- where
ground-truth generation is inexpensive (a few CPU-hours per system), so
that every protocol decision can be validated exhaustively; the
methodological questions it isolates --- constraint enforcement,
generalisation protocols, detection and its remedy, and uncertainty
screening --- arise identically in the higher-dimensional design spaces
where surrogate speed becomes a practical necessity.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{What to fit}: a systematic comparison of simplex-constraint
mechanisms --- six fraction-only heads plus a presence-supervised gated
head across five ternaries, against four classical baselines
(Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:remedy}) --- with a
\textbf{projection analysis} showing the renorm head's accuracy is fully
accounted for by post-hoc projection (Section~\ref{sec:closure}). Result:
presence supervision, not architecture, resolves the sharp-boundary
cases, and the regressor--detector gap it closes is stable across
presence thresholds (Section~\ref{sec:detection}).
\item \textbf{Where it holds}: two spatial generalisation protocols with
size-matched random controls and a region-matched reanalysis
(Section~\ref{sec:extrapolation}) --- contiguous interior-band holdout
(an in-hull shift, not extrapolation) and strict one-sided out-of-range
extrapolation --- separating spatial penalties from data-volume
reductions, complemented by a cheap ensemble-disagreement triage where
validated (Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty}).
\item \textbf{Why it matters}: a validated high-throughput application ---
a 501{,}501-composition lean-nickel screen with all 10{,}936 shortlist
points confirming under full CALPHAD, plus learning curves pricing
adoption (Section~\ref{sec:screen}) --- on top of a protocol re-run on
two extension systems and a four-point experimental anchor as supporting
evidence (Sections~\ref{sec:extensions} and~\ref{sec:anchor}).
\end{enumerate}
Failed mechanisms (sparsemax, sum-to-one penalty, residue closure)
delimit the scope at each step; the full evidence is in the Supplement
(Section~\ref{sec:negative}, Supplement Section~S1).

\section{Data generation}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Thermodynamic description}

The source database is the open MatCalc steel database \texttt{mc\_fe\_v2.062}
\citep{matcalc}, which cannot be parsed by pycalphad \citep{pycalphad} in its
raw form. We parse it with a lenient custom parser, extract the five
three-element subsystems, and apply minimal syntax repairs (reference-element
blocks and malformed function ranges). Both a pruned database (constituents
restricted to the system elements) and an unpruned database are built. Equivalence validation on 100 re-solved points
per ternary system confirms the pruning is bit-for-bit inert: maximum
$|\Delta\Np{\phi}| = 0$ and $|\Delta G_m| = 0$~J/mol for all five ternary systems.
All equilibrium calculations are performed with the open-source CALPHAD
implementation pycalphad \citep{pycalphad} against this database, so the
surrogates emulate this specific solver--database combination; their accuracy
is correspondingly bounded by the database's assessments and the solver's
convergence behaviour.

Eligible phase counts are 28 (Fe--Cr--Ni), 31 (Fe--Cr--Mn), 29 (Fe--Cr--Mo),
25 (Fe--Cr--V), and 29 (Fe--Mn--Ni); the two scope-extension systems use
the same parser and database, with 35 eligible phases for the quaternary
Fe--Cr--Ni--C subset and 32 for Fe--Cr--C. Pressure is fixed at 101{,}325~Pa
throughout.

\subsection{Probe-driven phase sets}

Running equilibria with all eligible phases costs ${\sim}2.5$~s per point.
To streamline dataset generation, a phase-set pre-screening is employed:
each system is independently probed with the full eligible set on 2{,}000
Dirichlet draws (RNG seed 7) to identify which phases are actively present,
and only phases observed active during this probing phase are retained as
regression targets, reducing the dimensionality $K$ from the total eligible
phases (Table~\ref{tab:systems}). The probe points are fresh Dirichlet
draws, independent of the dataset generated afterwards, so no label
from any train/validation/test row enters the phase-set construction. This
global preprocessing step fixes the output dimensionality for each system
prior to any predictive modelling. The fidelity of this phase-set reduction
is validated on an independent set of 1{,}500 points per system by
re-solving every point with the complete eligible phase set (25--35 phases
depending on system) and comparing per-phase amounts and total molar Gibbs
energies against the stored values. In four of the five ternary systems the
reduced-set and full-set equilibria agree to solver precision: $\max
|\Delta\Np{\phi}| \le 2.5\times10^{-9}$ and $\max |\Delta G| \le
7.8\times10^{-5}$~J/mol, and no excluded phase appears at any sampled
point. Fe--Cr--Mo is the exception: at 3 of 1{,}500 points (0.2\%), all
located in the Mo-rich, low-temperature corner ($x_{\mathrm{Mo}} \ge 0.42$,
$T \approx 700$~K) where BCC, $\mu$, and Laves phases compete closely, the
full-set solve returns a different assemblage involving LAVES\_PHASE at up
to $|\Delta\Np{\phi}| = 0.71$ and $|\Delta G| = 6.3\times10^{2}$~J/mol.
Every phase involved in either solution is already in the probe-active
target set, so these discrepancies reflect near-degenerate solver minima in
this region rather than an incomplete target set: repeated full-set solves
can return either minimum, and the exact failing-point count varies between
validation runs (1--3 of 1{,}500); no excluded phase exceeds the
$10^{-3}$ threshold anywhere. The same validation applied to the two
scope-extension systems passes at solver precision on Fe--Cr--Ni--C
($\max |\Delta\Np{\phi}| = 8.7\times10^{-9}$ over 1{,}500 points, no
point deviating by more than $10^{-3}$), while on Fe--Cr--C it exposes an
imperfection of the pre-screen: 9 of the 1{,}497 converged points (0.6\%)
return a different assemblage under the full 32-phase set, with $\max
|\Delta\Np{\phi}| = 0.95$ and $\max |\Delta G| = 36$~J/mol, the differing
entries involving BCC\_A2, BCC\_DISL, CEMENTITE, GRAPHITE, H\_BCC, and
M7C3; H\_BCC is never observed by the probe or the structured design, so
at these points the stored target vector is genuinely wrong. We keep the
rows and report the count rather than silently repairing the dataset: a
phase rare enough to evade a 2{,}000-draw probe is exactly the failure
mode this validation exists to catch. The target vector is the full probe-active
phase-fraction vector with no occurrence threshold, so $\sum_k y_k = 1$
holds to the solver's mass-balance tolerance: $|\sum_k y_k - 1| \le
3.3\times10^{-8}$ across all test rows.

Phases stable in multiple composition sets (for example the BCC
$\alpha/\alpha'$ miscibility gap) are summed over their composition-set
vertices into a single fraction column, matching the vertex accumulation
performed by the equilibrium solver. The pre-screening probe and the
structured design are different distributions over the Gibbs triangle: the
probe retains any phase observed at least once under uniform Dirichlet
draws, which is why MNNI2 --- active in only 0.1\% of probe draws and never
in the structured design --- enters the Fe--Mn--Ni target vector with zero
active rows. A phase stable in less than roughly 0.05\% of the design space
could evade both the 2{,}000-draw probe and the structured sampling and
thus be absent from the closed target basis.

The probe does, however, cover the \emph{entire} sampled domain, including
regions that later become test rows. Phase-set discovery is therefore a
global, whole-domain preprocessing step: the target dimensionality $K$ is
fixed with knowledge of the whole design space before any predictive split.
This is deliberate --- the surrogate's task is to predict fractions over a
known phase basis, and discovering that basis is part of building the
oracle interface, not part of the predictive task. It is not label
leakage in the ordinary sense (no test label is seen), but it does mean
that a phase active only in an unprobed region would be missed, and that
the reported accuracies are conditional on this globally predefined phase
basis being complete; the experiments do not assess discovery of previously
unseen phases (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Sampling and acceptance}

Design variables per ternary system are $(x_2, x_3, T)$ with
$x_{\mathrm{Fe}} = 1 - x_2 - x_3$, temperature uniform in $[700, 2000]$~K.
Five deterministic
sampling strategies (RNG seed 42) generate 11{,}220 raw design points:
6{,}000 Fe-weighted uniform draws, 2{,}000 sigma-field-focused draws, 720
grid points on five isothermal slices, 1{,}500 liquidus-zone draws, and
1{,}000 near-pure-Fe draws. A candidate is accepted only if the composition
lies inside the defined sub-simplex and the equilibrium satisfies mass
balance $|\sum_k \Np{k} - 1| < 10^{-6}$. Of the 11{,}220 raw design draws
per ternary system, 2{,}340 fell outside the sub-simplex and were discarded before
any equilibrium solve; of the remaining 8{,}880 valid candidates, 8{,}877
to 8{,}880 converged and satisfied mass balance, the only solver
rejections being three non-converged points in Fe--Cr--Mo. The 11{,}220 to
8{,}880 reduction is therefore a design-space filter rather than a
convergence filter, and the datasets contain no solver-driven holes.
Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarises the datasets
(Figure~\ref{fig:dataset} visualises the Fe--Cr--Ni case). The
scope-extension datasets below follow the same acceptance rule inside
their own design boxes.

The sampling distribution is deliberately structured, not uniform: the
five strategies over-represent the Fe-rich corner, the sigma field, the
liquidus zone, and isothermal grids. This is a design choice --- phase
boundaries and rare-phase regions carry most of the information a
surrogate must learn, and uniform draws would undersample them. The
consequence, which we keep in mind when interpreting results, is that all
reported accuracies are expectations under this distribution; rankings can
shift under a different prior (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Scope-extension datasets}

Two additional systems test whether the conclusions survive a change of
dimension and chemistry, under the identical probe, acceptance, and split
protocol. The \emph{quaternary Fe--Cr--Ni--C} system adds interstitial
carbon: solutes are drawn from $x_{\mathrm{Cr}} \in [0.001, 0.35]$,
$x_{\mathrm{Ni}} \in [0.001, 0.30]$, $x_{\mathrm{C}} \in [0.001, 0.05]$,
with six deterministic strategies: the five ternary strategies generalised
from the Gibbs triangle to an axis-aligned design box (uniform box draws,
low-alloy Fe-rich draws, isothermal grid slices, liquidus-zone draws,
near-pure-Fe draws) plus a high-solute low-temperature carbide-zone draw
replacing the ternary sigma-field draw --- yielding
13{,}200 raw candidates, of which eight fail the in-run gate (solver
exception or mass-balance violation) and 13{,}192 converge and pass mass
balance
($\max |\sum_k \Np{k} - 1| = 8.2\times10^{-9}$; 69.9\% of rows inside the
stainless box). The probe activates 9 of the 35 eligible phases, including
the three carbides M23C6, M7C3, and CEMENTITE and a GRAPHITE field. The
ternary \emph{Fe--Cr--C} system uses $x_{\mathrm{Cr}} \in [0.001, 0.30]$,
$x_{\mathrm{C}} \in [0.0005, 0.06]$ and the same strategies, yielding
13{,}199 accepted rows of 13{,}200 candidates (one fails the in-run
solver/mass-balance gate; $\max |\sum_k \Np{k} - 1|
= 6.8\times10^{-9}$, 100\% inside the box by construction), with 9 active
phases among 32 eligible, SIGMA appearing in only 0.4\% of rows. Both
datasets span $T \in [700, 2000]$~K. No hyperparameter search was
performed on either system; all heads and baselines use the fixed
protocol of Section~\ref{sec:models}. Together the seven datasets contain
70{,}788 mass-balance-validated equilibria; the extension systems are
reported in Section~\ref{sec:extensions}.

\section{Models and training}
\label{sec:models}

\subsection{Neural-network heads}

The base architecture is a multilayer perceptron with three hidden layers of
192 units (Linear $\to$ LayerNorm $\to$ SiLU $\to$ Dropout 0.1), trained with
AdamW (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $5\times10^{-4}$, cosine
annealing over 300 epochs, batch size 128, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0,
early stopping on validation MAE with patience 40). The loss is Huber with
$\delta = 0.01$ by default. Inputs are standardised with a
\texttt{StandardScaler} fitted on the training split only. Six output heads
are compared across the five ternary systems (Table~\ref{tab:main}); two further
mechanisms, power normalisation and a sum-to-one loss penalty, are
evaluated on Fe--Cr--Ni only as mechanism probes, reported in
Section~\ref{sec:ablations} (the penalty is revisited as a negative result
in Section~\ref{sec:negative}).

The \emph{sigmoid} head applies $\sigma(\mathbf{z})$ without normalisation
and serves as the unconstrained control. The \emph{softmax} head applies
$\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z})$. The \emph{sigmoid/$\Sigma$} head computes
$\sigma(\mathbf{z}) / \sum_k \sigma(z_k)$ at train time. The \emph{renorm}
head applies $\sigma(\mathbf{z})$ and then clips to $[0,1]$ and divides by
the row sum as a post-hoc projection; the projection lies outside the
training graph and is applied at inference time only. The \emph{sparsemax} head applies the
sparsemax operator \citep{martins2016sparsemax} to $4\mathbf{z}$, producing
exact zeros. The \emph{residue} head predicts $K{-}1$ fractions with
sigmoids and closes the sum algebraically, $\hat y_K = 1 - \sum_{k<K}
\hat y_k$; it enforces the sum-to-one equality by construction but does
\emph{not} intrinsically guarantee non-negativity, so it is not a fully
simplex-valid output (Section~\ref{sec:negative}). The two Fe--Cr--Ni
probes are the \emph{power-norm} head, $\sigma(\mathbf{z})^2 / \sum_k
\sigma(z_k)^2$ --- each sigmoid output raised to the power $p = 2$ and
renormalised by its row sum, applied inside the training graph --- and the
\emph{penalty} head, $\sigma(\mathbf{z})$ plus
$\lambda(\sum_k \hat y_k - 1)^2$ added to the loss.

We distinguish two properties that the literature often conflates.
\emph{Closure} is the equality $\sum_k \hat y_k = 1$ alone. \emph{Simplex
membership} is Eq.~\eqref{eq:simplex} in full: closure \emph{and}
$\hat y_k \ge 0$ for every $k$. Softmax, sigmoid/$\Sigma$, sparsemax, and
power-norm guarantee both; renorm guarantees both after its projection;
the residue head guarantees closure only.

Non-softmax heads use a per-phase two-layer output block ($192 \to 192 \to 1$)
and a learnable per-phase output scale. The softmax (and sparsemax) head is
therefore a single linear map from the shared trunk, while every other head
adds a per-phase block plus scale: capacity is not matched across heads, so
the softmax shortfall confounds constraint mechanism with capacity.

\subsection{Baselines}

Four classical models are fitted one output channel at a time on the raw
targets: ridge regression ($\alpha = 1$), $k$-nearest neighbours ($k = 10$,
distance-weighted), XGBoost \citep{chen2016xgboost} (500 trees, depth 8,
learning rate 0.05, early stopping at 50 rounds), and random forest (500
trees, depth 20) \citep{breiman2001random}. Each is evaluated raw and after
post-hoc renormalisation (clipped to $[0,1]$, then divided by the row sum;
clipping matters only for outputs that can leave $[0,1]$, since sigmoid
outputs already lie in $(0,1)$); the renormalised versions are the physically
admissible ones reported in the main comparison. All hyperparameters ---
neural and classical --- are fixed for the study: the comparison is intended
to isolate output-constraint and model-family behaviour under a fixed,
reproducible protocol rather than to identify globally optimal
hyperparameters for any model family. No model is tuned, and the tuning
lever is large: the width ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}) moves
renorm MAE by 28\% from $3\times64$ to $3\times256$ (0.0137 to 0.0099),
against a 5\% gap between the best heads (0.0112 for sigmoid/$\Sigma$,
0.0106 for renorm), so head rankings sit within tuning noise. No
Gaussian-process or radial-basis baseline is evaluated, although
three-dimensional inputs with $\sim$9{,}000 rows per system make that
family an obvious omitted comparator. Hybrid tree--neural comparisons for
phase equilibria have precedent in neighbouring domains
\citep{song2019clathrate}.

\subsection{Splitting, seeds, and metrics}

The split is cluster-stratified: for the ternary systems, KMeans
($k = 6$, seed-dependent
initialisation) on standardised $(x_2, x_3, T)$, with each cluster
contributing 64/16/20 train/validation/test by seed-dependent permutation.
The K-means stratification, the per-cluster shuffle, and hence the split
itself are re-drawn for every seed, so the reported seed-to-seed spread
reflects both split variation and training stochasticity.
Six clusters give a coarse stratification of the three-dimensional design
space while keeping each cluster large enough ($\sim$1{,}500 rows) for a
stable within-cluster permutation. (The scope-extension systems reuse this
machinery with the K-means fit on each system's full design-coordinate set
--- Section~\ref{sec:extensions}.) The validation partition is used only
for early stopping and XGBoost's stopping criterion; the test partition is
never touched during training or model selection, so all reported test
metrics are computed on data that played no role in fitting. Three
seeds (42, 123, 2024) control all stochastic stages; every reported number is
mean $\pm$ standard deviation over seeds, a measure of seed-to-seed training
stochasticity only, not a full uncertainty quantification (which would
additionally cover sampling, architecture, phase-set, and database
uncertainty). The test set is capped at 4{,}000
rows. The cluster-stratified split does not group near-duplicate rows:
dense sampling regions can place near-identical points on both sides of a
split boundary, so interpolation errors are optimistic for all models; no
grouped split is evaluated.

The primary metric is the mean absolute error averaged over phases,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K}
    \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |\hat y_{ik} - y_{ik}|.
    \label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}
Simplex closure is measured as $\mathrm{mean}\,|\sum_k \hat y_k - 1|$.
Phase-presence detection is assessed by per-phase $F_1$ and balanced accuracy
(active if $\Np{k} > 10^{-3}$); the reported mean $F_1$ is a macro-average
over the $K$ phases of each system. A phase with no
active row in a given test set (MNNI2, with zero active rows in the whole
Fe--Mn--Ni dataset) has an undefined $F_1$; we score it as $F_1 = 0$ for
every model, which keeps the phase in the average without favouring any
model. The $10^{-3}$
threshold is a practical choice: it sits two orders of magnitude below the
smallest non-trivial fraction a lever-rule calculation would report, and
above the solver's numerical noise floor ($\sim 10^{-8}$). Detection
conclusions are checked against this choice in
Section~\ref{sec:detection}, where the same metrics at $10^{-4}$ and
$10^{-2}$ leave the qualitative picture unchanged. An entropy-weighted MAE
weights rows by $1 + H(\mathbf{y})/H_{\max}$ with $H_{\max} = \ln K$,
up-weighting multi-phase coexistence states, where several non-zero
fractions must be predicted simultaneously; it is an auxiliary reporting
metric, not a theoretically privileged loss.

Fe--Cr--Mo. Simplex-valid outputs therefore do not guarantee phase-rule
admissibility, and this axis should accompany closure in future evaluation
protocols.

\section{Simplex closure}
\label{sec:closure}

Table~S3 of the Supplement reports the per-model mean closure violation
in full. All
structurally constrained heads satisfy $\sum_k \hat y_k = 1$ to
$\le 5\times10^{-8}$ --- float32 round-off over $K$ terms. The unconstrained
sigmoid violates closure by 6--8\% on average. Raw tree outputs violate it by
0.9--4.7\%. Post-hoc renormalisation repairs all evaluated models to machine precision
(${\sim}10^{-17}$).

Closure, however, is only half of Eq.~\eqref{eq:simplex}. The residue head
closes the sum by construction yet still emits negative fractions on
0.0--1.2\% of cells depending on the system (Section~\ref{sec:negative}),
so it is closure-valid but not simplex-valid. All other constrained heads
and the renormalised baselines satisfy both conditions.

\paragraph{The projection is where the accuracy comes from.}
The renorm head is, by construction, the sigmoid head plus a test-time
projection (Section~\ref{sec:models}). Projecting the stored raw sigmoid
predictions onto the simplex reproduces the renorm head's test MAE to
within $1.3\times10^{-4}$ in every system ($7\times10^{-4}$ per seed):
0.0107 against 0.0106 on Fe--Cr--Ni, 0.0081 against 0.0081 on
Fe--Cr--Mn, and so on (Table~\ref{tab:projection}, panel C). The entire
sigmoid--renorm gap in Table~\ref{tab:main} --- 0.0232 raw against 0.0106
renorm on Fe--Cr--Ni --- is therefore a scoring and constraint effect,
not an optimisation effect: evaluating an unconstrained model outside the
simplex is what costs accuracy, and the repair is a projection costing
microseconds at inference time. Part of the raw number is pure arithmetic:
a mean sum violation of 0.062 contributes at least $0.062/4 \approx 0.0155$
to raw MAE on this four-phase system, so most of the raw 0.0232 is the
closure violation itself rather than misplaced fraction mass. ``No accuracy
cost'' is therefore a statement against the raw sigmoid only, not against
an already-admissible model. The projection is likewise not an
accuracy lever for the already-admissible models: applied to the random
forest and XGBoost it changes MAE by at most $5\times10^{-4}$ (random
forest) and $2\times10^{-3}$ (XGBoost), small against the $0.0126$
raw-versus-renorm sigmoid MAE gap on Fe--Cr--Ni
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\paragraph{A family taxonomy of closure and membership.}
The baseline families occupy distinct positions relative to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:simplex} (Table~\ref{tab:projection}). Ridge regression is
a linear combination of target vectors that each sum to one, so its raw
outputs close the sum to $1.2$--$4.4\times10^{-10}$ --- machine
precision --- but a linear combination does not preserve
non-negativity: 16.6--27.7\% of cells are negative, the most negative at
$-0.50$. The random forest shows the mirror-image failure: it averages
non-negative per-channel predictions, so no cell is negative, but the
per-channel averages do not sum to one (closure 0.9--4.7\%). XGBoost,
whose per-channel models are unconstrained in both respects, fails both
(closure 1.6--4.1\%, negatives on 19.0--35.8\% of cells). The
unconstrained sigmoid never leaves $(0,1)$ but violates closure by
6--8\%. $k$-nearest neighbours, as a convex combination of complete
training label vectors, is simplex-valid a priori: closure better than
$5\times10^{-9}$ and no negative cell anywhere in the evaluated test
sets. This taxonomy describes the evaluated implementations, not intrinsic
family properties: the forest, XGBoost, ridge and $k$-NN arms each fit an
independent single-output regressor per phase channel
(Section~\ref{sec:models}), which is why the forest's per-channel averages
do not sum to one. A multi-output forest whose leaves store whole label
vectors would be simplex-valid by construction, like $k$-NN; it was not
evaluated here. Closure statistics alone therefore cannot certify admissibility;
the two conditions of Eq.~\eqref{eq:simplex} must be reported
separately.

The closure result has a practical consequence: a surrogate whose output
violates Eq.~\eqref{eq:simplex} cannot be used directly in any downstream
calculation that assumes a valid phase assemblage --- lever-rule property
averaging, for instance, or a subsequent kinetic simulation. The constrained
heads and post-hoc renormalisation both produce admissible outputs; the
former do so without a separate projection step. Differentiable
thermodynamic equilibria, which bake constraints into the solver itself,
are an emerging alternative not evaluated here
\citep{benhicham2026diffthermo}.

\paragraph{Rare phases are cheap in aggregate MAE and hard to detect.}
Aggregate MAE can underweight rare phases because the dominant zero-fraction
rows contribute almost no error: a phase present in 0.1\% of rows
contributes little to the average whether the model predicts it correctly
or not. Low MAE on such a phase is therefore not evidence that the model
represents it; it is an artefact of the averaging. Detecting its presence
--- the metallurgically important question --- requires the model to
distinguish a small positive signal from noise, which is what the
presence metrics measure. On Fe--Mn--Ni the MLP predicts MNNI, MNNI2, and
ALPHA\_MN as absent ($F_1 = 0$ for all three) while the random forest
resolves two of them. Missing such phases is not a numerical footnote:
intermetallic precipitation and sigma/carbide formation control real alloy
performance and service life \citep{rahnama2018precip,anburaj2012ageing,aditya2023sigma},
and Fe--Cr--Ni reliability is an active modelling target in its own right
\citep{hao2025mlip}.

\paragraph{The gap is not an artefact of the zero-positive scoring
convention.}
Excluding zero-positive phases from the macro-average raises the Fe--Mn--Ni
MLP $F_1$ only from 0.312 to 0.364, versus 0.504 to 0.588 for the random
forest. Threshold-free per-phase average precision (AUPRC) confirms the
pattern: across the ten rare phases with occurrence below 5\%, the renorm
MLP's average precision is 0.001--0.05 for nine of them (for example 0.014
for the 2\%-occurrence FCC phase in Fe--Cr--V and 0.003 for MNNI in
Fe--Mn--Ni; the exception is FCC in Fe--Cr--Mo at 0.63), whereas the random
forest retains 0.40--0.97 in all of them except the two near-zero-occurrence
phases (CR3MN5 at 0.03\% and MU\_PHASE\_I at 0.1\%), where both families
fail.

\paragraph{The gap is partly shaped by design choices.}
Three asymmetries favour the trees here and qualify the gap. First, the
Huber loss ($\delta = 0.01$) gives near-zero gradient to errors far below
$10^{-2}$, so tiny fractions contribute little training signal. Second, a
sigmoid output never reaches exactly zero while a tree leaf can, so at a
$10^{-3}$ presence threshold with MLP noise around $10^{-2}$, the
thresholded regressor comparison structurally favours trees. Third, no
dedicated classifier baseline is evaluated: detection is scored by
thresholding regressors, and the gated head that closes the gap sees
presence labels the other models never see. The gap is therefore a property
of this fixed-protocol comparison --- thresholded Huber-trained regressors
--- not a general statement that neural models cannot detect phases.

\paragraph{The gap is stable across presence thresholds.}
The presence threshold $\Np{k} > 10^{-3}$ is a modelling decision;
recomputation at $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-2}$ leaves the detector ordering
unchanged on four of five systems (Fe--Cr--Mo flips with the threshold),
so the gap is not a threshold artefact (Supplement Section~S2,
Table~S1).

\paragraph{Detection degrades faster than regression under shift.}
This gap widens under distribution shift. On the held-out temperature band of
Fe--Cr--Ni, the MLP's mean $F_1$ drops from 0.93 to 0.64 while its MAE rises
only from 0.010 to 0.013 (Section~\ref{sec:extrapolation}). Across all five
ternary systems and both model families, macro-$F_1$ falls by roughly 20--60\% in
relative terms on the held-out temperature bands, even as regression MAE
remains comparatively stable, whereas the composition bands affect
detection more mildly for the MLP (0.8--10\% relative) than for the random
forest (7--44\%). Phase-presence
detection is the first casualty of leaving the training distribution.

\subsection{Closing the gap: a presence-gated two-stage head}
\label{sec:remedy}

The regressor--detector gap has a standard remedy: separate the
presence decision from the fraction estimate. We evaluate it under the
same fixed protocol. A shared $3\times192$ trunk feeds two per-phase
heads: a \emph{fraction head} (per-phase sigmoid outputs trained with
inverse-prevalence phase-weighted Huber loss) and a \emph{presence head}
(per-phase logits trained with class-weighted binary cross-entropy at the
$10^{-3}$ presence threshold, $\mathrm{pos\_weight}_k =
\mathrm{clip}(n^{-}_k/n^{+}_k, 1, 100)$). The prediction is the
elementwise product $\sigma(\mathrm{frac}) \odot
\sigma(\mathrm{presence})$, followed by the clip-and-renorm projection at
evaluation (the \emph{gated} variant). An ablation variant
(\emph{weighted}) trains only the fraction head with the same phase
weights, isolating the contribution of the presence supervision. Three
seeds per system; no other hyperparameter was touched.

Table~\ref{tab:remedy} and Figure~\ref{fig:remedy} summarise the result.
Across the five ternaries the bare renorm MLP's macro-AUPRC spans
0.36--0.99 and the gated head's 0.91--0.99; the gated head beats the best of
the random forest, XGBoost, and $k$-NN on four of the five ternary systems
(Fe--Cr--V is a 0.988 against 0.990 near-tie, matching that system's

on all five ternaries, by 6\% (Fe--Cr--Ni) to 72\% (Fe--Mn--Ni, 0.0140 to

forest (0.0075 and 0.0055) though behind the best fraction-only head on

\emph{after} seeing the bare regressor fail on these same test sets, so
its test numbers are post-hoc by construction; and the shift evaluation
below covers two systems only. The presence
supervision, not the architecture, should therefore be read as the finding:
fraction-only rankings do not survive giving the MLP the labels the task
actually needs. The per-phase picture is where the remedy matters: on Fe--Cr--Mn
ALPHA\_MN rises from 0.004 to 0.933 and BETA\_MN from 0.005 to 0.896; on
Fe--Mn--Ni MNNI rises from 0.003 to 1.00. One rare phase resists --- the
0.1\%-occurrence MU\_PHASE\_I in Fe--Cr--Mo remains below 0.5 --- and is
reported as such. The weighted-only ablation is insufficient (macro-AUPRC
0.51--0.99, and on Fe--Cr--V it \emph{degrades} MAE by 28\%): the
presence supervision, not the loss reweighting, is the operative
ingredient.

\paragraph{On the two extension systems the gated head buys detection
at a measurable regression cost.}
On quaternary Fe--Cr--Ni--C the gated head raises macro-AUPRC from 0.805
to 0.965 against 0.860 for the best classical detector, and the macro over
the quaternary's carbide phases (M23C6, M7C3, CEMENTITE, GRAPHITE) from
0.661 to 0.947 --- CEMENTITE rises from 0.09 (MLP) and 0.17
(random forest) to 0.873, GRAPHITE from 0.58 to 0.926 --- at a 12\% MAE
cost. On Fe--Cr--C the picture is sharper: the bare MLP is a weak
detector (macro-AUPRC 0.559; CEMENTITE 0.036, SIGMA 0.003) while the
trees reach 0.896--0.897; the gated head reaches 0.922, lifting CEMENTITE
to 0.800, GRAPHITE to 0.808, and SIGMA to 0.805, and macro $F_1$ from
0.515 to 0.832, at a 47\% MAE cost. Whether that trade is worthwhile
depends on the application, but for alloy screening the metallurgically
consequential question --- will this carbide precipitate --- is exactly
what the bare regressor answers worst. Both extension systems are re-run
through the full protocol --- heads, spatial holdouts, and phase-set
validation --- in Section~\ref{sec:extensions}.

\paragraph{Under shift the remedy mostly holds --- except where detection
itself collapses.}

Fe--Cr--V) and gated AUPRC collapses to 0.59--0.63, the same detection
collapse the bare models show: the closed phase basis, not the head, is
the binding constraint there.

\section{Spatial generalisation: contiguous holdout and strict
extrapolation}
\label{sec:extrapolation}

\subsection{Two protocols, each with a random control}
\label{sec:protocols}

The cluster-stratified split of Section~\ref{sec:models} is an interpolation
test. We add two spatial protocols; in both the model is retrained from
scratch (same protocol, three seeds, four models, five ternary systems).

\emph{Contiguous interior-band holdout.} Two blocks are removed from
training and used as the test set: a temperature band $T \in [1200,
1400]$~K (1{,}275 rows) and a composition band $x_2 \in [0.25, 0.35]$
(1{,}676 rows). Because training rows remain on both sides of each band,
this is a missing-region generalisation test, not strict extrapolation. We
verified this geometrically: a Delaunay triangulation of the remaining
training points in scaled $(x_2, x_3, T)$ coordinates shows that
99.3--99.4\% of the held-out band rows lie \emph{inside} the convex hull of
the training set in all five ternary systems. The held-out region is a hole in the
sampling, not an out-of-support region.

\emph{Strict one-sided out-of-range extrapolation.} Training keeps only the
near side of one coordinate and the test set is the far side, with the
interior gap in neither split: train $x_2 \le 0.25$, test $x_2 > 0.35$
(2{,}679 test rows on Fe--Cr--Ni); and train $T \le 1200$~K, test $T >
1400$~K (3{,}219 test rows). Every test point now lies outside the training
range of the extrapolated coordinate by construction. This is strict
out-of-range extrapolation \emph{along one coordinate at a time}; it does
not imply that the whole multidimensional chemical space of the test rows
is unseen, since the other coordinates remain inside their training ranges.

The random controls are the key methodological element of both protocols.
Removing a region also removes training data, so a bare
region-versus-interpolation comparison cannot separate ``cannot generalise
there'' from ``had less to learn from''. Each block therefore has a
size-matched control: the same number of rows removed from training at
random, with an equal-size random test set. Any degradation beyond the
control is spatial. Because the control rows differ from the band rows,
these ratios alone conflate region difficulty with distribution shift. As a
complementary reference we therefore also report a region-matched
reanalysis: for each band, the band-holdout error is compared with the
error of the main-study models on the same band rows inside the
interpolation test set, where band data were present in training. This
ratio isolates the regional shift on identical rows; its residual confound
is a training-set size difference of roughly 15\%, since removing a band
also removes those rows from training. Table~\ref{tab:holdout} reports the band results and
Table~\ref{tab:extrap} the extrapolation results;
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} panel (a) shows the band penalty ratios and
Figure~\ref{fig:extrap} the extrapolation ones.

\subsection{Findings: contiguous interior-band holdout}

\paragraph{Two complementary references are needed for the band results.}
Results for the missing-region generalisation test (Table~\ref{tab:holdout},
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} panel~a) rest on two complementary references ---
the size-matched random controls and the region-matched reanalysis, both
defined in Section~\ref{sec:protocols}. Under the region-matched measure
(Table~\ref{tab:region}; ratio of mean MAEs on identical band rows) the
in-distribution-to-holdout penalty is smaller for the constrained MLP
than for the tree ensembles in nine of the ten band--system
combinations. On the composition bands, which cross steep
phase boundaries, the MLP penalty ratios are 1.2--2.1 versus 1.4--3.0 for
the random forest and XGBoost in all five ternary systems; on the temperature bands
the MLP ratios are 0.9--2.8 versus 1.3--3.9, with Fe--Cr--Ni the single
exception, where the random-forest ratio is lower (2.1 versus
2.8) although its absolute holdout error remains twice the MLP's
(0.0258 against 0.0132). The per-seed ratios in Table~\ref{tab:region}
show one instability behind the headline: on Fe--Mn--Ni the MLP
temperature ratios spread 0.9, 20.3 and 0.6 across seeds from a
near-zero denominator on one seed, which the ratio-of-means headline
(1.1) downweights.
Ratios must nonetheless be read alongside absolute errors: a band that is
easy in distribution, such as the nearly single-phase 1200--1400~K band of
Fe--Mn--Ni, inflates ratios through a small denominator.

\paragraph{The temperature-band penalty is mostly a data effect against
the random control, with a genuine shift residual on identical rows.}
On Fe--Cr--Ni the MLP and the random forest pay the \emph{same} penalty
factor of 1.31 once the random control is used as the reference. Comparing the
temperature band against the interpolation split instead would have
suggested that trees cannot generalise in temperature; the size-matched
control shows that the temperature band removes 1{,}275 training rows, and
the resulting data loss, not the spatial shift, accounts for most of the
degradation. The region-matched reference adds a residual: on the identical
Ni band rows, removing regional training data costs the MLP a factor of
2.8 (per-seed 2.7--2.9) against 2.1 for the forest. The two references
answer different questions --- the random control asks whether a
contiguous hole costs more than random removal, the region-matched
comparison asks whether the hole costs more on hard rows --- and on
Fe--Cr--Ni the second answer is yes.

\paragraph{The composition band is where model families separate.}
On Fe--Cr--Ni the random forest pays a factor of 3.11 on the composition band
against 1.61 for the constrained MLP (2.24 for XGBoost).
The same ordering holds on Fe--Cr--Mn (1.57 versus 0.85) and
Fe--Cr--Mo (1.85 versus 1.01). Since the held-out rows lie
inside the training convex hull, this is not an extrapolation result in the
strict sense; it is a result about graceful degradation under a contiguous
missing region. Tree-based models partition the input space into
piecewise-constant regions, so predictions inside the hole depend on the
learned partition boundaries and on how the forest aggregates across trees;
this can degrade rapidly when the hole spans a phase-boundary shift, while
the MLP's smooth parametrisation degrades more gracefully.

\paragraph{The composition-band ordering is consistent across all
system--seed pairs.}
As a statistical check on the spatial protocol, we applied a two-sided
sign test with the system as the independent unit: seeds within a system
share the same dataset, so seed-level pairs are not independent. On the
composition band the MLP's penalty is lower than the random forest's and
lower than XGBoost's on all five systems ($n = 5$, $p = 0.062$ for each
comparison; all 15 system--seed pairs agree). On the temperature band the
MLP--forest ordering holds on three of five systems ($p = 1.0$),
consistent with the shared penalty factor of about 1.3, while the
MLP--XGBoost ordering holds on all five systems ($p = 0.062$).

\paragraph{The penalty is system-dependent, not universal.}
On Fe--Cr--V the random forest degrades by a factor of 4.92 on the temperature band
--- a phase-boundary-rich region where the forest's local averaging fails
badly. On Fe--Mn--Ni the held-out bands are \emph{easier} than the random
control (0.04--0.73 times the control), because the bands sit in the dataset's easy,
liquid-dominated corner. Generalisation difficulty is a property of the
system and the region, not of the model alone.

\paragraph{Detection collapses on the temperature band.}
Mean $F_1$ drops sharply for all four evaluated models on the temperature
band, even where
MAE barely moves. On Fe--Cr--Ni the MLP's $F_1$ falls from 0.93 (control) to
0.64 (temperature band); the random forest's from 0.85 to 0.53. The
phase-presence decision is more sensitive to distribution shift than the
fraction regression.

\subsection{Findings: strict one-sided out-of-range extrapolation}

\paragraph{All evaluated model families degrade sharply beyond the training
range.}
On Fe--Cr--Ni the composition-extrapolation MAE rises to $\sim$0.17 for the
MLP and $\sim$0.22 for the random forest, against $\sim$0.013 on the
matched control --- an order of magnitude. Temperature extrapolation is
similarly severe. No architecture evaluated in this study extrapolates phase
fractions reliably beyond the trained composition or temperature range;
this is the honest headline of the strict protocol.

\paragraph{The temperature protocol is additionally limited by the closed
phase basis.}
Liquid has no active row in the near-side training data ($T \le 1200$~K) in
all five ternary systems, so the far side demands a phase that the target basis
cannot express. In
Fe--Mn--Ni this renders the task nearly uninformative: all four model
families land within 4.1\% of the constant near-side-mean predictor (MAE
0.182--0.184 versus 0.190), and the per-phase errors show that the unseen
liquid column alone contributes 0.088 of the 7-phase mean, its entire
far-side mean fraction of 0.613 being missed. In the other systems the
models still beat the constant predictor substantially (for example
0.099--0.197 in Fe--Cr--V versus 0.307 for the constant predictor),
indicating partial transfer of the seen-phase structure. The composition
protocol, by contrast, keeps every frequent phase inside the training basis
(only trace phases such as ALPHA\_MN, BETA\_MN, and MNNI on the Mn-rich far
side of Fe--Mn--Ni are unseen) and therefore measures genuine spatial
extrapolation.

\paragraph{The constrained MLP reaches the lowest far-side error.}
In absolute terms the constrained MLP's far-side MAE is the lowest of the
three model families on composition extrapolation in all five ternary systems and
on temperature extrapolation in all five ternary systems (Figure~\ref{fig:extrap},
panel b). Relative to its own matched control the MLP's penalty ratio can
be \emph{higher} than the trees' --- the trees' controls are themselves
degraded, so the ratio is reference-dependent --- and we report the
absolute-error comparison as the cleaner statement. A two-sided sign test
over the 15 system--seed pairs agrees with that caveat: the MLP's
penalty ratio is lower than the trees' on only 9 of 15 pairs
($p = 0.61$), so the ratio comparison is not significant. Seeds within a
system are not independent, so the system-level reading is 6 of 10
system--protocol cells favouring the MLP --- the same non-significant
picture. The ordering matches
the contiguous-band result: smooth parametrisations degrade more
gracefully than leaf averaging when the query leaves the trained region.
The difference is one of degree, not of kind --- graceful degradation is
not reliable extrapolation.

\section{Uncertainty screening}
\label{sec:uncertainty}

A deployment needs to know when \emph{not} to trust the surrogate. We
evaluate two per-row scores on the five ternary systems, both computed
from artefacts the protocol already produces.
\emph{Ensemble disagreement} (U1) is the per-row standard deviation across
the three seed models' fraction predictions, averaged over phases; the
ensemble prediction itself is the seed mean. \emph{Input-space distance}
(U2) is the distance from the query point to its nearest training row in
standardised $(x_2, x_3, T)$ coordinates --- the intuition being that
error should grow away from training support.

\paragraph{Ensemble averaging is a refinement; its disagreement is a
usable error ranker on most systems.}
Averaging the three seeds reduces test MAE by 2.26\% on average
($-0.62\%$ on Fe--Cr--Mo to $+5.53\%$ on Fe--Cr--V) when every test row
contributes the mean over whichever seeds cover it. That union estimate
mixes one-, two- and three-seed partial ensembles; the fully paired
comparison on the rows covered by all three seeds gives a mean gain of
0.35\% (0.39, 0.40, 0.89, 0.06 and 0.00\% per system), on only 75--78
common rows per system. All contributing predictions are out-of-sample for
their seed (each seed's stored predictions cover its own test rows only),
so there is no train--test contamination; the gap between the two estimates
is partial-ensemble mixing plus small-$n$ noise, not leakage. The honest
reading is that the ensemble gain is small and uncertain under redrawn
splits: ensembling is not a fix for any failure mode, but it is free
accuracy, and a fixed-split rerun is needed to pin the number down
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). As a per-row score,
however, U1 carries real signal. Its Spearman correlation with per-row
error averages 0.707 across the five ternary systems (0.94 on Fe--Cr--Ni, 0.93 on
Fe--Cr--Mn, 0.87 on Fe--Cr--V, 0.69 on Fe--Cr--Mo, but 0.11 on
Fe--Mn--Ni), and the AUROC for flagging rows whose mean absolute error
exceeds 0.05 averages 0.695 (0.89, 0.86, 0.61, 0.73, and 0.39
respectively). The operational form is a coverage--risk curve: retaining
the 50\% of rows with the lowest U1 cuts MAE from 0.0123 to 0.0029 on
Fe--Cr--Ni (a factor of 4.3), from 0.0090 to 0.0023 on Fe--Cr--Mn
(a factor of 3.9), and from 0.0147 to 0.0073 on Fe--Cr--Mo (a factor of 2). On
Fe--Cr--V and Fe--Mn--Ni the curve is flat or reversed (0.0160 to
0.0192; 0.0145 to 0.0196), so the gate is not universal: it should be
validated once on a held-out split per system --- a cheap check ---
before being trusted in a screening loop.

\paragraph{Input-space distance has no power; disagreement is not a shift
detector.}
The nearest-training-row distance does not rank error at all: mean AUROC
0.408, below the 0.5 of a random score. This is not paradoxical but
structural. Under the interpolation split every test row \emph{is} close
to training rows --- that is what interpolation means --- while the rows
that are hardest are those near phase boundaries, where error is driven
by local target complexity rather than by distance from support
(Section~\ref{sec:fields}). Nor can a disagreement score replace the
spatial protocols of Section~\ref{sec:extrapolation}: three models trained
on the same data can agree confidently and wrongly wherever the training
set carries no information, and no in-sample score detects that. The
division of labour is straightforward: U1 for cheap per-row triage inside
the trained region, the holdout protocols --- or direct CALPHAD
revalidation --- anywhere else.

\paragraph{Huber and MAE losses are equivalent; MSE is worse.}
The Huber loss with $\delta = 0.01$ and the MAE loss give statistically
indistinguishable results ($0.0106$ versus $0.0103$ for renorm). The MSE
loss is 47\% worse ($0.0156$), because rare-phase outliers dominate the
squared error.

\paragraph{Accuracy improves monotonically with width up to 256.}
Widening the hidden layers from 64 to 256 units reduces the renorm MAE from
$0.0137$ to $0.0099$. The default $3\times192$ architecture was fixed
before this ablation was run; the ablation is a post-hoc diagnostic, and
architecture search was deliberately out of scope for this study. We
therefore keep 192 as the default in all main comparisons rather than
retroactively adopting 256, and report the ablation so that readers can
judge how much accuracy an architecture search could still recover. The
reported MLP configuration should therefore not be interpreted as
architecture-optimal.

\paragraph{The closure penalty is strongly detrimental.}
Adding $\lambda(\sum_k \hat y_k - 1)^2$ to the loss raises MAE to $0.0804$
at $\lambda = 1$ and $0.1693$ at $\lambda = 10$ --- factors of 8 and 16
over the default renorm head's $0.0106$ --- while reducing
the closure violation only from $6.2\times10^{-2}$ to $1.3\times10^{-3}$.
For the tested penalty formulation and $\lambda$ values, the soft closure
penalty substantially degraded MAE and did not achieve the numerical
closure obtained by structural constraints. The penalty is redundant ---
the targets already sum to one --- and competes with the data loss; related
physics-informed superalloy workflows report the same constraint--data-loss
tension \citep{karthik2025sumrule}. The
penalty weights were restricted to $\lambda \in \{1, 10\}$: with the Huber
regression term saturating its per-output gradient at $\delta = 0.01$, these
weights make the closure term dominate the regression gradient by one to
two orders of magnitude, and no smaller $\lambda$, gradient-rescaled
penalty, or warm-up schedule was evaluated. The defensible conclusion is
therefore that the soft penalty adds nothing over the structural heads at
the tested weights, not that soft penalties are unviable in general.

\paragraph{Power normalisation does not beat renormalisation.}
The power-norm probe (Section~\ref{sec:models}) reaches $0.0123 \pm
0.0014$ MAE on Fe--Cr--Ni --- worse than the renorm head's
$0.0106 \pm 0.0006$, though far better than the raw sigmoid's
$0.0232 \pm 0.0010$ (Table~\ref{tab:main}). Squaring each sigmoid output
before row-sum renormalisation reweights the distribution toward larger
fractions without improving on plain renormalisation at the tested
setting.

\section{Constraint mechanisms that fail}
\label{sec:negative}

Three constraint mechanisms that are plausible in principle fail in
practice under the evaluated protocol. Each failure delimits the scope of
the positive results above rather than qualifying them: the main text
states each boundary, and the Supplement (Section~S1, Figure~S1) reports
the full evidence.

\paragraph{Sparsemax: sparsity at the cost of stability.}
Sparsemax \citep{martins2016sparsemax} satisfies closure to
$\le 5\times10^{-8}$ with exact zeros, but it exhibited severe seed
sensitivity and substantially worse MAE: 2--4 times worse than renorm or
sigmoid/$\Sigma$, with a seed standard deviation of the same order as the
mean (0.018/0.037/0.117 on Fe--Cr--Mo, for example) --- consistent with a
zero-gradient sparsity trap, and tested for one implementation and
optimisation configuration only. Dedicated sparsemax losses and entmax
variants remain untested.

\paragraph{Sum-to-one penalty: redundant and harmful.}
The targets already sum to one, so a soft sum-to-one penalty competes with
the data fit: MAE rises to $0.0804$ at $\lambda = 1$ and $0.1693$ at
$\lambda = 10$ (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) while closure improves only to
$1.3\times10^{-3}$. Full curves are in Supplement Figure~S1.

\paragraph{Residue closure: closure without non-negativity.}
Closing the sum algebraically ($\hat y_K = 1 - \sum_{k<K} \hat y_k$)
guarantees the equality but not non-negativity: the residue head emits
negative fractions on 0.0\% (Fe--Cr--V) to 1.21\% (Fe--Cr--Mo) of cells,
worst cell $-0.59$, and is 1.4--2.5 times worse than the best constrained
head in every system, with the dropped phase best chosen as the dominant
one in four of five systems. Any deployment would need a projection step
after all.

\section{Scope extensions: quaternary Fe--Cr--Ni--C and Fe--Cr--C}
\label{sec:extensions}

The preceding sections were developed on five ternaries with three
inputs (Section~\ref{sec:remedy} already reports the gated head on both
extension systems). This section re-runs the full protocol --- probe-driven
phase set, fixed heads and baselines, identical split and seeds (for the
quaternary the K-means fit uses all four design coordinates ---
$x_{\mathrm{Cr}}$, $x_{\mathrm{Ni}}$, $x_{\mathrm{C}}$, and temperature ---
with the same $k = 6$ and seeds 42, 123, 2024), no hyperparameter search
--- on the two scope-extension datasets of
Section~\ref{sec:data}, to test whether the head ranking, the
regressor--detector gap and its remedy, and the spatial protocols
survive a change of dimension (three to four inputs, with interstitial
carbon) and a change of chemistry (carbide-dominated phase diagrams).

Table~\ref{tab:extension} reports the results in full. Three findings
stand out.

\paragraph{The smooth-coexistence ranking survives four inputs.}
On quaternary Fe--Cr--Ni--C the renorm head achieves $0.0037$ MAE
against $0.0075$ for the random forest --- a twofold margin, larger
than on any ternary --- with sigmoid/$\Sigma$ at 0.0042 and softmax at
0.0054; on Fe--Cr--C the sigmoid/$\Sigma$ head achieves 0.0029 against
0.0055 for the forest. Both systems are dominated by extended smooth
coexistence fields (austenite/ferrite/liquid with M23C6 and M7C3
two-phase regions), and the smooth function class of the MLP wins
decisively. The axis-aligned trees did not recover their advantage in
four dimensions, and ridge regression remains an order of magnitude
worse (0.081--0.082). With only two extension systems this is evidence,
not proof, that the ternary geometry--ranking correspondence extends to
four inputs.

\paragraph{The detector gap widens exactly where phases get rarer, and
the gated head transfers without retuning.}
The quaternary renorm MLP is a competent detector overall (macro-AUPRC
0.805) but fails on the two rarer carbides, and on Fe--Cr--C it collapses
to 0.559 macro-AUPRC --- the bare regressor essentially does not see
phases occurring below about 2\% of rows, while the trees retain
0.56--0.97.
The presence-gated head, trained with the identical recipe used on the
ternaries, lifts these systems to 0.965 and 0.922 macro-AUPRC
(Section~\ref{sec:remedy}): the remedy transfers with no retuning, at the
regression cost quantified there. The phase-set pre-screen passes at
solver precision on the quaternary system and shows the nine documented
Fe--Cr--C deviations (Table~\ref{tab:extension} panel D;
Section~\ref{sec:data}); neither the accuracy nor
the detection results depend on repairing them, and none were repaired.

\paragraph{The spatial protocols give the same qualitative answers.}
Composition-band holdout costs 2.1--3.5 times the random control and
strict extrapolation fails on every tested axis, worst in temperature
(Table~\ref{tab:extension} panel C); the absolute-error ordering of
Section~\ref{sec:extrapolation} --- no architecture extrapolates
reliably --- does not depend on being ternary.

\section{Application: high-throughput stainless screening}
\label{sec:screen}

The preceding sections validate the surrogate as an emulator; this one
uses it as a tool. We screen Fe--Cr--Ni at $T = 1000$~K --- below the
$\sim$1300~K liquid appearance, at a service-relevant temperature --- for
a lean-nickel austenitic window: fully austenitic
(NP$_{\mathrm{FCC}} \ge 0.99$), sigma-free
(NP$_{\mathrm{SIGMA}} \le 10^{-3}$) and melt-free
(NP$_{\mathrm{LIQUID}} \le 10^{-3}$), with nickel capped at 12~mol\%
(Ni is the cost driver; the design task is the least Ni that still holds
austenite). The screen evaluates 501{,}501 compositions on a
$10^{-3}$ simplex grid with the three-seed renorm ensemble of
Section~\ref{sec:anchor} (0.4~s on CUDA, 0.27~$\mu$s per point per seed),
shortlisting 10{,}936 hits (2.18\%).

\paragraph{Every shortlisted alloy checked validates; the frontier is a
Ni--Cr trade-off curve.}
Figure~\ref{fig:screen} maps the screen. The shortlist hugs the lean edge
of the austenite field; per-Cr-bin minimal-Ni points trace a Pareto
frontier falling from 7.5\% Ni at 1.5\% Cr to 4.8\% Ni near 12--13\% Cr
before rising again as sigma encroaches --- Cr substitutes for Ni as the
austenite stabiliser up to $\sim$12\%. All 11 frontier points validate
under the full 28-phase set at CALPHAD FCC $= 1.0000$, as do all 400
points of a uniform random hit subset --- and a follow-up run validated
the remaining 10{,}525 shortlist points with zero misses and zero solver
failures: 10{,}936 of 10{,}936 shortlisted alloys confirm, so the filter
wastes no validation run. A 400-point uniform
background sample bounds the other side: 76 fall in the design scope
(Ni $\le$ 0.12), of which 4 CALPHAD-qualify while the screen rejected
them. Three sit within 0.01 of a decision cutoff (two rejected on
predicted sigma just over $10^{-3}$, one on FCC $0.9838$ with sigma also
over) ---
expected boundary flips for a model with $\sim$0.01 MAE --- and one is a
clear model miss (Fe 0.927/Cr 0.010/Ni 0.063: predicted FCC 0.9006,
CALPHAD 1.0). The screen is conservative by construction: it errs toward
exclusion, and its misses cluster at the decision boundary. Treating the
in-scope background miss rate (4 of 76) as representative, recall in scope
is $\sim$0.8: the filter finds most qualifying alloys and certifies the
ones it returns.

\paragraph{Disagreement flags the worst miss.}
The screen records ensemble spread (U1) per point. All four in-scope
misses sit above the shortlist median spread ($2.5\times10^{-5}$), and
the clear model failure carries $1.0\times10^{-2}$ --- ten times the
shortlist 95th percentile --- for an AUROC of 0.86 separating misses
from hits (Figure~\ref{fig:operating}, panel~a; $n = 4$ misses, so this
is suggestive, not conclusive). A gate at the shortlist 99th percentile
would have excluded that failure while retaining 99.0\% of the
shortlist. The uncertainty score of Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty} thus
earns a screening role: not a universal error ranker, but a shortlist
triage gate.

\paragraph{The query choice is characterized, not asserted; the worst
miss sits in a coverage hole.}
Varying the FCC cutoff (0.97/0.99/0.995) against the sigma cutoff
($10^{-4}$/$10^{-3}$/$10^{-2}$) on the 811-point validation pool gives
precision 1.00 at all nine combinations, with pool recall from 0.80
(strictest) to 1.00 (loosest); the deployed query sits at precision 1.00
and recall 0.99 (Figure~\ref{fig:operating}, panel~b;
Table~\ref{tab:screen}). Training-set density around the misses completes
the picture: the clear failure sits 1.6 times farther from its nearest
training row than the validated-hit median (0.166 against 0.102 in
standardized coordinates), while the three boundary flips sit at or
below the median --- a coverage hole behind the one real error,
threshold edges behind the rest.

\paragraph{One screen amortises the pipeline several times over.}
Measured on this workload, a full-set single-point solve costs 0.41~s
wall (3.30~s CPU over 8 workers) and a probe-set solve 0.33~s wall;
training the three screened seeds cost 268~s total. The one-time cost is
dominated by data generation (8{,}880 accepted equilibria): at the
measured 0.33~s per probe-set point the break-even screen size is
$\sim$10$^4$ points, against the 2.5~s prose full-set figure
$\sim$7$\times$10$^4$ points. The demonstration screen ran 501{,}501
points --- a payback of roughly 7 to 50 times in a single query, before
counting reuse. The honest boundary of this claim: one temperature, one
system, and validation itself spent 11{,}336 full-set solves ($\sim$80~min wall,
0.23--0.82~s per point across chunks: boundary points cost more).
Screening is cheap; trusting the screen still requires spot
CALPHAD, and the numbers above say how much spot-checking suffices.

\paragraph{Half the data buys most of the accuracy.}
Retraining the screened head on fractions of its training split, under the
identical protocol, gives test MAE 0.0231 at 10\% (568 equilibria),
0.0173 at 25\% (1{,}420), 0.0121 at 50\% (2{,}840) and 0.0091 at 100\%
(5{,}681), seed-means over the same three seeds
(Figure~\ref{fig:screen}, panel~c; Table~\ref{tab:screen}). Half the
data-generation bill reaches within a factor of 1.3 of full-data accuracy,
which halves the optimistic break-even above; even 568 solves train a
0.023-MAE surrogate, roughly the raw-sigmoid baseline. The 25\% point
carries a caveat --- one seed stalls at 0.0217 while the other two reach
0.015 --- so small-data training is seed-sensitive and wants the same
multi-seed discipline as the main benchmark. The full-data retrain lands
at 0.0091 against Table~\ref{tab:main}'s 0.0106: retrain-to-retrain
variation from early stopping, of the same order as the seed spread, and
one more reason the headline comparisons use three seeds.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{When do constrained MLPs beat trees?}

In the five ternary systems studied here, the answer tracked geometry. Systems with
extended smooth coexistence fields --- Fe--Cr--Ni and Fe--Cr--Mn --- favoured
the smooth function class of the MLP, with Fe--Cr--Mo a statistical tie
between the families. Among the systems dominated by sharp, essentially
binary phase boundaries, Fe--Mn--Ni favoured axis-aligned tree splits,
whereas Fe--Cr--V, another sharp-boundary system, is a statistical tie
between the families. We present
this as an empirical observation across the tested systems, not a universal
law: with only seven systems (five ternaries and two extensions,
Section~\ref{sec:extensions}), system identity is confounded with phase
topology, rare-phase frequency, sampling density, and boundary geometry ---
and all five ternaries share the same seed-42 sampling template
(Section~\ref{sec:data}), so coverage is confounded with system
identity too. No quantitative sharpness metric is evaluated, so the
topology story is a hypothesis consistent with the results, not a measured
result. Crucially, the ranking above is conditional on fraction-only
supervision: the presence-supervised gated head leads the forest on all
five ternaries (Table~\ref{tab:main}). A surrogate pipeline should
therefore evaluate both families \emph{and} the presence-supervised variant
and select per system; no single fraction-only architecture consistently
dominated across the five ternary systems.

\subsection{The spatial-generalisation result reframes the interpolation
comparison}

Section~\ref{sec:extrapolation} reframes the main comparison: the MLP's
advantage is not merely better in-distribution fit but less severe
degradation when queries leave the trained region --- relative, not
reliable, extrapolation. Temperature-protocol numbers are joint failures
of basis (liquid unseen near-side in all five ternaries) and
generalisation. For alloy-design workflows querying unseen compositions,
the relative comparison is the more relevant one.

\subsection{What the surrogate is and is not}

These results frame the surrogate as a high-speed screening filter rather
than a thermodynamic arbiter: while the MLP speedups enable the screening
of multi-million composition databases in seconds, any candidate alloy
identified during high-throughput screening \citep{mayr2009screening} must be independently
re-validated using direct CALPHAD calculations and, ultimately, targeted
experimental synthesis. Measured on CPU, the 192-unit MLP forward pass
costs 2--3~$\mu$s per point in batch inference (0.4--0.6~ms for a single
query), against roughly 2.5~s per equilibrium on the CALPHAD side of this
work --- a speedup of about $10^{6}$ in batch mode and
$4$--$6\times10^{3}$ for single queries. The 2.5~s denominator is the full
eligible phase set (28--31 phases); the probe-reduced set actually used for
data generation costs less per point (unrecorded), so the factor against
the as-run pipeline is smaller. Break-even against data generation
(70{,}788 equilibria) plus training time is worked through for the
demonstration screen in Section~\ref{sec:screen}
($\sim$10$^4$--7$\times$10$^4$ points); any new deployment must redo that
accounting for its own query volume.

The surrogate emulates the oracle: for a queried $(\mathbf{x}, T)$ it returns
the phase fractions the database's equilibrium routine would return, fast.
It inherits the oracle's assumptions --- the database's Gibbs energies, the
equilibrium assumption itself, and the solver's convergence behaviour. It is
not an independent thermodynamic statement. ``Validated against the database''
here means ``reproduces the database's answers to solver precision on the
validated points'', not ``thermodynamically true''. The experimental anchor
of Section~\ref{sec:anchor} bounds what this distinction costs in
practice: on four published DTA transition temperatures the surrogate
stays within 21~K of the full-CALPHAD answer (mean 17.75~K at the
$10^{-4}$ detection threshold the protocol uses) while the database
itself deviates from experiment by 19~K on average (median 8~K) --- the
same order of magnitude, with the surrogate term larger on three of the
four points. The $10^{-2}$-threshold figure of 1--3~K describes tail shape,
not the headline comparison, and both terms are reported separately rather
than conflated.

\subsection{Limitations}

Phase-set sufficiency is validated on 1{,}500 re-solved points per system,
not proven
for the whole design space; a phase active only inside an unprobed region
would be missed, and the phase basis itself was fixed with knowledge of the
whole sampled domain, so all results are conditional on this globally
predefined phase basis and do not assess discovery of previously unseen
phases (Section~\ref{sec:data}). The closed phase set also bounds what the
strict extrapolation protocols can test: liquid lies outside the near-side
basis in all five ternary temperature-extrapolation systems, so part of the
far-side error there is a basis limitation. The contiguous-band holdout
is a missing-region test inside the sampled domain; the strict
extrapolation protocol is one-sided and covers only one coordinate at a
time, and behaviour beyond the sampled simplex remains unvalidated. The
sparsemax and penalty conclusions are scoped to the tested hyperparameters.
The design distribution is deliberately structured, not uniform, so all
reported average errors are expectations under that sampling distribution;
rankings could shift under a different prior. All MLPs share one fixed
architecture and all baselines use fixed hyperparameters; the comparison is
a fixed-protocol benchmark, not an exhaustive hyperparameter search, and
the reported MLP is not architecture-optimal. The mean $\pm$ standard
deviation over three seeds quantifies seed-to-seed training stochasticity
only, not total predictive uncertainty. Per-experiment seed counts are
small; the sparsemax variance claim in particular rests on three runs. The
phase-boundary analysis uses an
empirical boundary proxy, not the exact thermodynamic distance to a phase
boundary, and the proxy inherits the sampling density of the design;
computing true boundary distances from a dense CALPHAD grid would decouple
the two. Finally, the comparison set is not
exhaustive over function classes: classical low-dimensional interpolants
such as radial-basis-function or spline fits, or Gaussian-process
regression, might approximate these three-dimensional phase-fraction fields
competitively, and we did not evaluate them; the $k$-NN baseline partially
covers the local-smoothness regime. Compositional-data-analysis regressors
such as log-ratio (ALR/CLR/ILR) regression and Dirichlet regression are
likewise not evaluated \citep{meloni2026coda,parent2025nutrition}: the targets contain structural zeros --- typically only one
to three of the $K$ phases are active on any given row --- and log-ratio
transforms are undefined where any component is zero, so these families
would need ad hoc zero replacement before fitting. The presence-gated
remedy is evaluated at one architecture, one presence threshold, and two
loss variants; it leaves one rare phase (MU\_PHASE\_I in Fe--Cr--Mo)
undetected and costs 12--47\% MAE on the two extension systems,
so it is a demonstrated remedy, not a tuned optimum. The uncertainty gate
is validated on five ternary systems and fails on two of them; the seed
disagreement score must be checked per system before use. The experimental
anchor comprises four points from two studies against one database, one
of which (the most Cr-rich alloy) deviates by 59~K --- it bounds the
surrogate's contribution but cannot validate the database. The Fe--Cr--C
pre-screen deviation (9 of 1{,}497 points) is a known imperfection of the
probe-based phase-set construction at rare-phase margins, kept in the
released dataset. Three further design limitations bound generality. All
five ternaries share one seed-42 sampling template --- identical
stainless-box thresholds (38.0\% in-box on every system) and identical
sigma-focus boxes, including on Fe--Mn--Ni, where no sigma phase is ever
active and the draw has no metallurgical meaning --- so system identity is
confounded with sampling coverage. Label noise is documented but not
removed: near-degenerate solver minima in Fe--Cr--Mo and nine genuinely
wrong Fe--Cr--C target rows mean part of the boundary-concentrated error
may be solver disagreement rather than model error, and no second-solver
cross-check is performed. Physical admissibility stops at the simplex: no
lever-rule mass-balance or phase-rule check is applied to predictions, the
gated head is tested under shift on two systems only (with one
composition-extrapolation reversal on Fe--Cr--V), and the ensemble gain is not
re-estimated on a fixed split. All conclusions are furthermore scoped to systems of at
most four inputs: the MLP/tree crossover, the band-holdout behaviour, and
the regressor--detector gap are tested up to the quaternary system, and
whether they persist in genuinely higher-dimensional design spaces,
where axis-aligned tree partitions lose their advantage, remains
untested.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We benchmarked simplex-constraint mechanisms for phase-fraction
prediction --- six fraction-only heads plus a presence-supervised gated
head, against four classical baselines, on five Fe-based ternaries
(44{,}397 equilibria) plus quaternary and carbide extensions (26{,}391
more; 70{,}788 in total): a systematic empirical benchmark with controls,
not a new architecture.

Structurally constrained heads --- softmax, sigmoid/$\Sigma$, and renorm ---
satisfy the full simplex constraint (closure and non-negativity) to float32
precision at no accuracy cost relative to the unconstrained sigmoid, which
violates closure by 6--8\%. The residue head enforces closure by
construction but not non-negativity, emitting negative fractions on up to
1.2\% of cells. Post-hoc renormalisation repairs all evaluated models to machine
precision at no accuracy cost and is also \emph{sufficient} --- projecting
the unconstrained sigmoid reproduces the renorm head's accuracy to
$1.3\times10^{-4}$ MAE everywhere --- while closure alone certifies
nothing (ridge closes to $10^{-10}$ with up to 28\% negative cells;
$k$-NN is the only baseline simplex-valid a priori).

No single fraction-only architecture consistently dominated across the five
ternary systems; performance is topologically dependent. Within the five
ternary systems studied, constrained MLPs led the two systems dominated by
continuous, smooth phase coexistence fields, the random forest led
Fe--Mn--Ni --- one of the two systems defined by sharp, binary phase
boundaries --- and the remaining two systems (Fe--Cr--Mo and Fe--Cr--V)
were statistical ties --- a 2--1--2 pattern across the five ternary
systems --- as paired-bootstrap tests confirmed. That pattern is
conditional on fraction-only supervision: the presence-supervised gated
head leads the forest on all five ternaries
(Table~\ref{tab:main}), so the durable finding is that presence
supervision, not family selection, resolves the sharp-boundary cases.

Two spatial protocols with matched controls separated spatial penalties
from data-volume penalties. On contiguous interior holdout
(99.3--99.4\% in-hull, so distribution shift rather than extrapolation)
the random forest paid a factor of 3.11 against 1.61 for the constrained
MLP on the Fe--Cr--Ni composition band, whereas the temperature-band
penalty is mostly a data effect against the random control. Under strict
one-sided out-of-range extrapolation all evaluated model families
degraded sharply, the MLP least in absolute error --- graceful
degradation, not reliable extrapolation --- with temperature numbers
jointly limited by the closed phase basis. Band-limited spatial shifts
are tolerable for constrained MLPs, whereas one-sided extrapolation
should be treated as unsupported and re-validated with direct CALPHAD
evaluation, and any workflow in which rare phases matter should
complement the regressor with the presence-gated head of
Section~\ref{sec:remedy}.

Phase-presence detection degrades faster than regression under distribution
shift, and the best regressor is not necessarily the best detector --- but
the gap closes: the presence-gated head raises macro-AUPRC to 0.91--0.99
on all seven systems (best classical detector beaten on six) while
reducing MAE on the five ternaries (12--47\% cost on the two extension
systems); presence supervision, not reweighting, is what works --- and the
remedy survives missing-region shift and composition extrapolation on the
two tested systems, failing only where detection collapses for all models
(Section~\ref{sec:remedy}).

Two further results complete the operational picture. A three-seed
disagreement score ranks per-row error (mean AUROC 0.70) and gates MAE by
up to a factor of 4.3 at 50\% coverage on the systems where it helps,
whereas input-space distance has no power (AUROC 0.41). And the protocol transfers: on the two
extension systems constrained MLPs lead the forest by factors of
1.9--2.0, the spatial protocols reproduce the
same qualitative penalties (temperature extrapolation remains
15--20 times the control on the extension systems, subject to the same
closed-basis limitation documented for the ternaries), and against four
published DTA transition points the
surrogate matches the full-CALPHAD answer within 21~K (mean 17.75~K at the
$10^{-4}$ threshold) --- the same order as the database's own 19~K mean
deviation from experiment, with the surrogate term larger on three of the
four points. Finally, the surrogate screens: a 501{,}501-composition
lean-nickel query at 1000~K shortlists 10{,}936 candidates in 0.4~s with
all 10{,}936 shortlist points confirming under full-set CALPHAD
(Section~\ref{sec:screen}) --- the screening-filter claim, demonstrated
rather than asserted.

Three failed mechanisms delimit the scope. Under the evaluated configuration
sparsemax exhibited severe seed sensitivity, consistent with a
zero-gradient sparsity trap. For the tested formulation and $\lambda$
values, a sum-to-one penalty was redundant and strongly detrimental. The
residue head, which closes the sum algebraically, is 1.4--2.5 times
worse than the best constrained head in all five ternary systems, consistent with the
residual phase absorbing the accumulated error of the other channels.
Section~\ref{sec:negative} states each boundary; the full evidence is in
the Supplement, Section~S1.
